Phosphatidylserine Customs Bond Review for US Importers

Learn when U.S. phosphatidylserine imports need a customs bond, how single transaction and continuous bonds differ, and what the bond does not replace.

Published August 18, 2026

عرض منتجات PSطلب المواصفات

The short answer is that most commercial phosphatidylserine imports into the United States should be reviewed as customs-bond shipments before the goods depart, not after they reach the port. As of the CBP help articles published on February 13, 2026 and checked for this article on Tuesday, August 18, 2026, CBP says a customs bond is required for commercial imports worth more than $2,500 or for a commodity subject to other federal agency requirements. For a food or dietary-ingredient shipment such as PS, the practical question is therefore usually not whether customs compliance exists at all, but which bond structure fits the shipment and who should own it.

A customs bond is also not just a payment detail. Under 19 CFR 142.4 and 19 CFR 113.62, the bond supports release from CBP custody and secures duties, taxes, charges, and other entry obligations. If the entry summary is late or other obligations are not met, the bond can become the enforcement lever.

That is why this topic belongs in the purchase and booking workflow. A buyer can have the right product, a clean COA, the right HS code review, and an organized ISF filing plan, yet still create avoidable delay if the bond principal, bond type, and filing structure were never settled.

This article explains what U.S. buyers should check before the first PS shipment or the next repeat order.

What a U.S. Customs Bond Does and Does Not Do

For a normal commercial import, the relevant instrument is the basic importation and entry bond on CBP Form 301. Under 19 CFR 113.62, that bond may be either a single transaction bond or a continuous bond. The same section also explains why buyers should not treat the bond as a simple tariff payment tool. It secures:

  • deposit of estimated duties, taxes, and charges within the prescribed time;
  • payment of additional duties, taxes, and charges later found due;
  • completion of entry documentation where release occurs before final completion;
  • other basic importation and entry conditions tied to the transaction.

This matters because the bond sits inside the legal mechanics of entry, not outside them. If a PS shipment is released and the required filing or payment steps are not completed correctly, the bond is one of the ways CBP protects the revenue and enforces compliance.

The bond does not replace the other workstreams around the shipment. It does not decide the final classification, it does not substitute for a country-of-origin marking review, and it does not answer FDA-specific food import questions. Buyers who treat the bond as “the broker’s paperwork” usually discover too late that the bond touches ownership, cash exposure, filing discipline, and redelivery risk.

When a Phosphatidylserine Shipment Usually Needs a Bond

The governing regulation is 19 CFR 142.4. It says merchandise generally may not be released from Customs custody at the time entry documentation is received unless a single entry or continuous bond on CBP Form 301 has been filed. The same section provides a narrow waiver path for some low-value cases: the port director may waive surety or cash deposit when the merchandise value secured by the bond does not exceed $2,500, the entry summary is filed and estimated duties are deposited before release, and the importer has not been delinquent or otherwise remiss in Customs transactions.

That waiver is real, but buyers should not turn it into an assumption. The same rule makes clear that the waiver does not apply to quota merchandise and may be denied where classification, appraisal, or redelivery concerns exist. CBP's public help guidance, checked on August 18, 2026, states the practical public-facing standard even more simply: customs bonds are required for commercial imports over $2,500 or for commodities subject to other agency requirements.

For PS buyers, the commercial decision usually falls into one of these lanes:

Shipment situationLikely bond questionWhy the question appears
First commercial trial shipmentSingle transaction bond or continuous bond?Importer may not know whether the route will repeat
Reorder program with multiple entriesIs a continuous bond now more practical?Repeated entries make transaction-by-transaction bonding inefficient
Low-value sample or test lotCan the shipment move informally or under a waiver path?Value, entry type, and agency flags matter
Importer-of-record changeWhose bond should support the entry and summary?The filing party and principal must line up
High-scrutiny route or compliance concernWill CBP demand extra security?19 CFR 113.13 lets CBP require more protection if needed

The point is not to guess. The point is to settle the bond structure before the goods are on the water.

Single Transaction Bond vs Continuous Bond

For PS buyers, this is the operational core of the topic.

CBP's public help materials updated on February 13, 2026 say that single transaction bonds are generally for the value of the merchandise plus duties, taxes, and fees, while continuous bonds usually equal 10% of the duties, taxes, and fees paid in the previous 12 months, with a public minimum of $50,000 noted in CBP's bond guidance. Those public formulas are useful planning rules, but they do not override CBP's authority under 19 CFR 113.13 to judge sufficiency and require additional security where needed.

For a buyer, the choice is usually strategic:

Bond typeBest fitMain advantageMain caution
Single transaction bondFirst shipment, occasional import, one-off projectLimits the bond to one entry instead of the whole year's activityCan become repetitive and slower if the route becomes regular
Continuous bondRepeating orders, multiple ports, recurring entries, broader import programOne standing bond can support multiple transactionsPrincipal should monitor sufficiency, because CBP can review and demand increases

That means the right question is not “which bond is cheaper?” The stronger question is how stable the U.S. import program really is. If your team is testing one supplier and one customer program, a single transaction bond may be commercially sensible. If you expect repeat PS entries, multiple SKUs, or combined imports across ingredients, the case for a continuous bond becomes stronger.

The distinction also affects workflow. Under 19 CFR 113.11, continuous bond applications go to the Director, Revenue Division and must describe the general character of the merchandise plus the prior or estimated duties and taxes. Under 19 CFR 113.12, continuous bonds must be approved by the Revenue Division, and only one continuous bond for a particular activity is authorized for each principal. A buyer who waits until the container is already booked may discover that the operational timeline was unrealistic.

Bond Sufficiency Is Not a One-Time Decision

Many buyers assume the bond decision is finished once a surety issues the form. The regulations do not treat it that way.

19 CFR 113.13 says CBP reviews bond sufficiency and considers factors such as the principal's payment history, compliance history, the value and nature of the merchandise, the type of supervision CBP will exercise, and the principal's record in honoring bond commitments. The same section also says CBP may require additional security immediately if it believes the revenue is in jeopardy or enforcement would otherwise be hampered.

That has practical consequences for PS importers:

  1. A continuous bond sized for a small first-year import program may become inadequate when annual order volume grows.
  2. A shipment with classification or admissibility complications may draw a different security conversation than a routine reorder.
  3. The bond principal should monitor changes in who files the entry, who files the entry summary, and who owns the goods at entry.

This is one reason finance, customs, and procurement should talk to each other before a repeat-order calendar is locked. The customs valuation guide explains why entered value matters in the entry file; the bond review explains why that same value profile can also affect the security conversation.

The Bond Does Not Replace FDA Food-Entry Steps

A PS buyer can easily confuse four different U.S. obligations:

RequirementWhat it answersDoes the bond replace it?
Customs bondWhat secures the importation and entry obligations?لا
Customs entry and entry summaryWhat is being entered, classified, valued, and released?لا
FDA entry informationIs the product an FDA-regulated food or dietary ingredient and what data supports review?لا
FDA prior noticeHas FDA been notified in advance of imported food?لا
FSVP importer controlsHas the importer established supplier-verification procedures for the food route?لا

FDA's current import pages make the separation clear. The FDA Entry Submission page says that food products require prior notice and entry information, and it explicitly lists dietary supplements and dietary ingredients in that food lane. FDA's prior notice page says prior notice must be provided for all food for humans and animals imported or offered for import into the United States.

So the customs bond is necessary in many PS shipments, but it is never the whole answer. A bond-secured entry can still fail on:

  • missing or incorrect prior notice;
  • incomplete FDA entry data;
  • a weak FSVP importer file;
  • poor alignment between invoice description, technical specification, and entry classification;
  • route confusion over the manufacturer identity or food facility registration support file.

That is why strong import files separate responsibilities instead of blending them into one vague “import paperwork” folder.

The Importer-of-Record Question Should Be Settled Early

The bond review also forces one uncomfortable but useful question: who is actually the U.S. principal for the entry?

In many PS projects, the commercial buyer, the consignee, the FSVP importer, and the entry filer are not the same party. That can work, but it must be structured carefully. 19 CFR 142.4(b)(2) specifically addresses the case where entry is made in the name of one party and supported by that party's bond, while the entry summary is later filed in the name of another. In that case, the party named in the entry summary must file a bond of the required type.

The operational takeaway is straightforward:

  • do not assume the broker's process solves the principal question automatically;
  • do not let the commercial invoice name, consignee structure, and entry-summary party drift apart without review;
  • lock the importer-of-record model before the vessel sails.

This is also where the ISF guide and bond review connect. The ISF importer, customs entry filer, and bond principal may work together in one transaction, but they do not answer the same legal question.

A Practical Buyer Checklist Before Freight Is Booked

Use this sequence before the first commercial PS shipment or the next importer-of-record change:

  1. Lock the product identity. Confirm source route, grade, assay basis, packaging, and invoice description.
  2. Lock the U.S. principal. Identify the importer of record, customs broker, consignee, and FSVP importer if different.
  3. Check whether the route is occasional or repeating. That often drives the single-versus-continuous-bond decision.
  4. Confirm classification and entered-value logic. Bond discussions become weak when the commercial file is still moving.
  5. Confirm FDA lane requirements. Prior notice and FDA entry data should be scoped at the same time, not after the bond is arranged.
  6. Confirm timing for the bond application. Continuous bonds need setup time; do not assume same-day readiness.
  7. Record the filing rule. Note who will file entry, who will file entry summary, and which bond supports each step.
  8. Create a repeat-order trigger. Reassess the bond if annual import volume, principal, or route changes materially.

That checklist is narrow on purpose. It keeps the bond in its own lane while still connecting it to the rest of the import file.

الأسئلة الشائعة

Do U.S. importers usually need a customs bond for phosphatidylserine?

Often yes for commercial imports. CBP's public help guidance checked on August 18, 2026 says a customs bond is required for commercial imports worth more than $2,500 or for commodities subject to other federal agency requirements. Because PS is imported as a food or dietary-ingredient route, buyers should review the bond question before shipment instead of assuming the lane is informal.

Is a single transaction bond better for a first PS order?

It can be, especially for a true one-off or first commercial shipment. A single transaction bond is often the cleaner choice when the buyer has not yet decided whether the import program will repeat. If the route becomes regular, a continuous bond may become more efficient.

When does a continuous bond make more sense?

Usually when the importer expects repeated PS entries, multiple ports, or a wider ingredient import program. CBP's public guidance says continuous bonds are usually based on 10% of the prior 12 months' duties, taxes, and fees, subject to a public minimum and CBP sufficiency review.

Does a customs bond replace prior notice or FDA entry information?

No. FDA's import pages treat dietary supplements and dietary ingredients as food products for entry and prior-notice purposes. The bond secures CBP importation and entry obligations; it does not substitute for prior notice, FDA entry data, or FSVP responsibilities.

Can the entry be supported by one party's bond and the entry summary filed by another?

That situation can occur, but 19 CFR 142.4(b)(2) says that if the entry is made in one party's name and the entry summary is later filed in another party's name, the party named in the entry summary must file the required bond. Buyers should not leave this question unresolved with the broker.

What happens if the entry summary is filed late?

19 CFR 142.15 says that if the entry summary documentation is not filed on time, the port director shall make an immediate demand for liquidated damages in the entire amount of the bond in the case of a single entry bond. For a continuous bond, the demand is measured as if the merchandise had moved under a single entry bond.

Conclusion

The U.S. customs-bond decision for phosphatidylserine is not a clerical afterthought. It is part of the entry design for the shipment. Buyers should decide early whether the route fits a single transaction bond or a continuous bond, confirm who the bond principal really is, and keep that decision separate from classification, prior notice, FDA entry data, and FSVP ownership.

The practical rule is simple: if the shipment is commercial, cross-border, and expected to move through a formal U.S. entry workflow, review the bond before the goods depart. That is faster than fixing the principal, the filing structure, or the security amount once the shipment is already under broker pressure.
