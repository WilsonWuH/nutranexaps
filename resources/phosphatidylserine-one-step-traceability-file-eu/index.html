Phosphatidylserine One-Step-Back Traceability File for EU Buyers

Build a one-step-back and one-step-forward phosphatidylserine traceability file for Europe with supplier, lot, dispatch, customer, and recall records.

Published August 22, 2026

View PS ProductsRequest Specifications

Lot traceability is more than printing a code on a drum. EU food law requires traceability at all stages and expects operators to identify the immediate supplier and the businesses supplied. For a PS importer or distributor, the file must connect the material received to the supplier, the transport event, the internal status, and every downstream customer or location that received it.

For EU importers, distributors, contract manufacturers, and quality teams, whether a lot can be located and controlled during a complaint, hold, or recall depends on evidence that is specific enough to the quoted material and practical enough for the team that will receive, formulate, release, or sell it. The buyer should separate three questions: what the law or customer requires, what the supplier controls, and what the importer or finished-product operator must decide. Keeping those questions separate prevents a generic statement from being treated as a product approval.

Direct answer: Maintain a controlled one-step-back and one-step-forward file for each commercial PS lot. At minimum, link the supplier and manufacturing site, product and source, lot and package identifiers, quantity received, transport and receipt date, status decision, quantities dispatched, customer or warehouse destination, and the documents used for release. A lot code is a key, not the whole record; it must resolve quickly to evidence.

Define the Review Before Requesting Evidence

Start with a written scope statement. Record the product name, soy or sunflower route, assay or grade as shown on the current controlled document, physical form, carrier or other declared components, manufacturing site, package, destination, and intended use. If any of these fields are unknown, the review is not yet about a defined PS lot. It is about an assumption, and assumptions should be visible before they enter a purchase order or customer file.

The scope also determines which evidence is relevant. Supplier identity, dispatch and receiving records, lot-to-drum mapping, quantity reconciliation, warehouse movements, customer dispatch records, COA/specification, complaints, and recall contacts should be linked to the same product identity. A document from another grade, another source, or an earlier site may be useful background, but it should not silently become release evidence. Ask the supplier to identify the document owner, revision, effective date, and affected lots whenever the file is used for a commercial decision.

Why the Buyer Question Matters

The commercial risk is usually an evidence mismatch rather than a lack of paperwork. A buyer can have a specification, a COA, a transport record, and a supplier statement and still be unable to answer whether a lot can be located and controlled during a complaint, hold, or recall because the records use different names or do not share a lot key. The review should therefore test the connections between documents, people, packages, and decisions, not count the number of attachments in an email.

The operational risk is also different from the legal risk. A rule may require a record, while the warehouse needs a label and the formulation team needs a grade-specific specification. Treating all three as one generic compliance request creates friction and encourages teams to copy old files. Use the buyer question as the organizing principle, then request only the evidence needed to reach a documented decision.

A Practical Control Workflow

A practical control sequence for this topic is to define the lot key and data owner; capture supplier and receipt data at intake; reconcile each drum or pallet to the internal inventory record; capture downstream dispatches; test the retrieval process with a timed mock exercise. The sequence should identify a responsible person at each handoff. Procurement can define the commercial request, quality can define the evidence and release rule, regulatory can map the market requirement, and logistics can preserve the transport or package record. The same person may hold more than one role in a small organization, but the file should still show who made which decision.

Use a go, hold, or escalate logic. Go when the exact product is identified, the required evidence is current and lot-linked, and the responsible reviewer has approved the decision. Hold when a key field is missing, inconsistent, or unsupported. Escalate when the event may affect safety, regulatory status, customer release, or a wider set of lots. The logic should be agreed before an exception occurs, because pressure at the dock or production line is a poor time to invent a procedure.

When a supplier response is incomplete, write a focused question rather than asking for all compliance documents. Request the missing field, explain the decision it supports, and state whether the material remains on hold. This makes the supplier conversation more efficient and creates a useful audit trail. It also lets Nutranexa technical support respond to the exact quoted PS route instead of sending a generic document pack.

Review the evidence at the same time as the commercial milestone. A new source should be reviewed before the sample is approved; a shipment issue before receipt release; and a process or packaging change before the affected lot is used. If the review is postponed until a customer complaint or border question appears, the team may no longer be able to reconstruct the original assumption.

Buyer checklist

  • Freeze product identity and source route before comparing documents.
  • Record the exact grade, package, and lot identifier shown on the controlled file.
  • Confirm destination market and intended application before choosing a comparison.
  • Request the current specification and COA and check revision, date, and lot linkage.
  • Name the quality owner and define go, hold, and escalate conditions.
  • Keep supplier change and escalation contacts in the quality file.
  • Record unanswered questions, not only the supplier's final answers.
  • Reconcile the decision with the purchase order, receiving record, or production packet.

Record Review and Release Discipline

The record should tell a reviewer what happened without requiring a private conversation with the original buyer. Include the request, product identity, documents reviewed, open questions, decision owner, decision date, and next review trigger. For this topic, retain the lot traceability matrix, receiving and dispatch documents, quantity reconciliation, electronic or paper scan history, customer notice list, exercise result, and any corrective actions. The record can summarize confidential supplier evidence rather than copying every page, but it should make the source and revision of the summary clear.

If the decision depends on a test, sampling event, or physical inspection, preserve the relationship between the sample and the lot. Pass is not a useful record when the sample cannot be identified, the method or comparison is absent, or the report belongs to a different grade. If the evidence is qualitative, say what was observed and what limitation remains. A clear limitation is safer than an unsupported assurance.

Buyer Scenarios and Common Failure Modes

Consider a typical buyer handoff. Procurement sends the supplier a request, quality reviews the response, logistics receives the shipment, and a formulation or customer team asks whether the material is ready. The file succeeds when each team can see the same product identity and status. It fails when procurement uses a forecast name, the supplier uses a production code, logistics uses a pallet number, and quality receives a COA with no matching key. The corrective action is not more attachments; it is a controlled identity and handoff.

A second scenario is a change discovered after approval. The supplier may issue a revised document, a new packaging format, or a new test result. The buyer should compare the new evidence with the approved baseline and decide whether the event is routine, requires verification, or requires requalification. Do not let the presence of a current date make the change disappear. Date and revision show when a document exists; they do not show whether the approved product remains the same.

The most common avoidable mistakes are using a purchase-order number as the only lot identifier, mixing source lots under one internal code, tracking pallets but not drum-level labels, failing to record partial dispatches, or keeping customer records in a sales mailbox with no controlled backup. Each one removes context from the final decision. Add a short exception field to the checklist so the reviewer can state what was not available, who accepted the limitation, and what follow-up is required. That is especially important when a distributor passes a file to a contract manufacturer or when a customer asks for evidence under a different market rule.

FAQ

Is a PS lot number enough for EU traceability?

No. The lot number must resolve to supplier, receipt, inventory, dispatch, and customer records. Without those linked records it is only an identifier, not a usable traceability system.

Do distributors need to track each drum?

The level should be sufficient to locate and control the affected material. For bulk PS, drum, pallet, lot, and partial-dispatch relationships should be defined in the operating procedure.

What is one-step-forward traceability?

It is the ability to identify the immediate businesses or locations supplied by the operator. Keep customer, quantity, lot, and dispatch records in a retrievable form.

How often should a buyer test traceability?

Use a periodic mock retrieval and repeat it after system, warehouse, ERP, supplier, packaging, or product-route changes. The procedure should set the response expectation and corrective-action path.

Should traceability include the COA?

Yes, where the COA supports lot release or customer communication. Link the controlled document to the exact lot and version rather than storing an unrelated reference report.

Buyer handoff: test retrieval before an incident

An EU traceability file should be tested with a lot that can be identified in the warehouse and in the supplier's controlled records. Select a drum or pallet, record the lot and package identifiers, retrieve the supplier document and COA, identify the immediate upstream source, and trace the quantity into the receiving, formulation, or customer record. Record the time, missing fields, duplicate entries, and any manual step that would slow a hold or withdrawal decision.

The exercise should end with an action list, not only a pass or fail label. Assign owners for missing links, set a due date, and repeat the test after an ERP, supplier, package, warehouse, or route change. Keep one-step-back and one-step-forward records distinct so the team knows whether it is locating the source or the recipients. A controlled mock retrieval also helps the buyer explain exactly which lot evidence can be supplied during a customer question without claiming that every downstream record sits with the ingredient manufacturer.

Conclusion

The safest way to handle phosphatidylserine one-step-back traceability is to define the exact material, connect the relevant evidence, and document the decision owner before the commercial milestone. Use the checklist to separate supplier evidence from importer or finished-product responsibility, keep unresolved gaps visible, and escalate when the evidence cannot support whether a lot can be located and controlled during a complaint, hold, or recall.
