Phosphatidylserine South Africa: SAHPRA Import Guide for B2B Buyers | Nutranexa

A sourcing-level guide to importing phosphatidylserine into South Africa — SAHPRA licensing, DisCom listing, COA and document requirements, customs steps.

Published November 3, 2026

View PS ProductsRequest Specifications

South Africa is the anchor market for nutraceutical demand across sub-Saharan Africa, and cognitive-health formulations are among its fastest-growing categories. For a brand or contract manufacturer planning a phosphatidylserine product for this market, the sourcing conversation is shaped less by marketing claims than by two questions: can the ingredient dossier clear SAHPRA expectations, and can the supplier's document set survive a SARS customs inspection at Durban or OR Tambo? This guide walks through both, from importer licensing to the certificate of analysis you should insist on. Before any paperwork begins, it pays to lock the material itself — grade, concentration and botanical source — against a defined specification such as the phosphatidylserine ingredient profiles published by Nutranexa, because every downstream registration and customs step references that specification.

How South Africa Regulates Supplement Ingredients: SAHPRA and DisCom Listing

South Africa regulates health supplements and complementary medicines through the South African Health Products Regulatory Authority (SAHPRA), under the Medicines and Related Substances Act. Unlike markets where a supplement ingredient enters under a purely food-style notification, SAHPRA treats many complementary medicines — including cognitive-health and brain-supplement categories where phosphatidylserine typically sits — through a structured listing and registration system. For most finished products, this means a DisCom submission in SAHPRA's online e-registration portal, where the product is listed against its discipline and category before it may be marketed.

For a B2B buyer, the practical consequence sits upstream of the finished-product listing: the raw-material dossier must be complete, traceable and consistent. SAHPRA reviewers and auditors look for a named manufacturing site, a defined specification, and identity and purity data for the actual material sold. This is why buyers who source PS powder from a factory-direct supplier with an established quality and R&D documentation programme tend to move through listing and customs with fewer queries — the same dossier serves registration, customer questionnaires and port inspection alike.

One point worth confirming early: whether the finished product will be positioned as a complementary medicine or whether the brand intends a food-style presentation, because the evidence expectations and labelling rules differ. Whatever the route, the ingredient specification does not change, so build the raw-material file once and reuse it.

Licence, Importer of Record and Customs Steps for PS Powder

Two entities matter on every shipment: the licensed importer of record and the clearing agent acting at the border. The importer must hold the relevant licence to bring health products or supplement ingredients into South Africa, and the product listing must align with what is declared to SARS. A mismatch between the listed product name, the invoice description and the material in the drum is one of the most common causes of delayed PS shipments.

Customs paperwork itself is conventional: commercial invoice, packing list, bill of lading or air waybill, certificate of origin, and the HS classification for the phosphatidylserine preparation. Duty and VAT treatment depends on the declared classification and any applicable trade-agreement preference, so the clearing agent should confirm the tariff treatment before the vessel sails rather than at the terminal. Buyers planning first shipments into this market can shorten the learning curve by reading the site's guide to importing PS into India under FSSAI, which follows the same logic of licence-first, document-consistent shipments — the sequence in South Africa is analogous even though the regulator differs.

The Document Set a PS Supplier Must Deliver for SAHPRA-Aligned Sourcing

The document pack a South African buyer should request before issuing a purchase order is not exotic — it is the same core set that disciplined buyers demand in the US and EU, applied consistently:

  • Certificate of analysis, lot-specific — covering PS assay (typically by HPLC), peroxide value, moisture, heavy metals and microbiology, signed by the manufacturer's QC unit.
  • Specification sheet — the registered standard the COA is tested against, including physical description and tolerances.
  • Allergen, GMO and origin declarations — critical for soy-derived PS and decisive for sunflower-derived grades.
  • GMP or ISO manufacturing certification for the producing site, plus, where relevant, third-party audit summaries.
  • Stability or retest data supporting the shelf life claimed on the drum label.
  • SDS and label copy matching the transport and warehouse conditions of a phospholipid powder.

A useful discipline is to treat the pack as an audit object, not a formality. The site's walkthrough of COA red flags during supplier audits applies directly here: inconsistent assay methods between COA and specification, missing lot numbers, or a COA issued before production dates are exactly the details a SAHPRA-aligned buyer should challenge before shipment, not after.

Sourcing Decisions: Soy vs Sunflower PS for the South African Label

Both soy-derived and sunflower-derived phosphatidylserine are viable for the South African market; the choice is commercial and label-driven rather than regulatory. Soy PS remains the cost-efficient workhorse at scale, while sunflower PS supports soy-free and allergen-light positioning, which is an increasingly common claim in urban retail channels. What matters for the dossier is consistency: whichever botanical source you list in the DisCom submission must match the declarations in the document pack, and any supplier-initiated source change afterwards is a change notification that reaches the listing. Buyers weighing the trade-offs can compare the two grades side by side in the site's soy vs sunflower PS sourcing guide, which sets out cost, allergen and documentation differences relevant to any market.

FAQ

Is phosphatidylserine approved as an ingredient in South Africa?

Phosphatidylserine is used in complementary medicine and health-supplement presentations internationally, and South African buyers should confirm the product's category and listing route with SAHPRA's current guidance and a local regulatory consultant. The ingredient itself is characterised by a defined specification, and the buyer's job is to ensure the raw-material dossier supports whatever presentation the finished product claims.

Does the importer or the foreign supplier hold the SAHPRA licence?

The South African importer of record holds the licence to bring regulated products into the country. The foreign manufacturer's role is to supply the manufacturing, quality and certification documents that support the importer's listing and the shipment's customs declaration. This division is why supplier responsiveness on documents is a genuine selection criterion, not a nice-to-have.

Can I import PS powder for contract manufacturing before my finished product is listed?

B2B ingredient importation for manufacturing and the listing of a consumer-facing product are separate processes, and the sequence should be confirmed with the clearing agent and regulatory adviser before the first commercial order. In practice, buyers align the raw-material specification and document pack first, because those artifacts feed both tracks.

What sample size should I request for South African market validation?

A pilot quantity of 1–5 kg with a lot-specific COA is usually sufficient for formulation trials, blend-uniformity checks and stability initiation, and it mirrors the sample-evaluation practice described in the site's bulk-approval checklist. Order commercial volumes only after the trial material and its documents behave as expected.

Conclusion

South Africa rewards the same sourcing discipline as every mature regulated market: define the specification, verify the document pack, and keep the licensed importer, the listing and the customs declaration telling one consistent story. For a phosphatidylserine programme specifically, the two decisive relationships are with a supplier whose COAs survive scrutiny and a clearing agent who understands health-product shipments.
