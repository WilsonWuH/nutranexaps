Phosphatidylserine Supplier Food-Safety Certificates: A European Buyer Review

Review ISO 22000, FSSC 22000, and HACCP evidence for a phosphatidylserine supplier: scope, validity, verification, and what certificates do not prove.

Published August 3, 2026

View PS ProductsRequest Specifications

European buyers sourcing bulk phosphatidylserine (PS) are routinely asked to accept "ISO certified" or "HACCP certified" suppliers. Those phrases are useful starting signals, but they are not a finished qualification. A certificate only proves what its scope, validity period, issuing body, and audit records say, and none of those details can be assumed from a logo or a one-line claim in a quotation email.

The direct answer is: EU food law does not require a non-EU ingredient supplier to hold a specific voluntary certificate such as ISO 22000 or FSSC 22000, but the EU hygiene rules make HACCP-based procedures a legal baseline for food business operators, and European buyers should review any claimed certificate the same way they review a COA: check the exact scope, the site, the standard version, the validity period, the certification body, and the verification trail before connecting it to a specific PS batch. A certificate supports supplier qualification; it never replaces specification review, batch COA review, or the importer's own legal responsibilities under EU food law.

This resource gives European procurement and quality teams a practical certificate-review workflow for phosphatidylserine suppliers: the EU legal baseline, the voluntary certificates buyers actually see, how to read scope and validity, and how to verify a certificate before tying it to a product and batch.

The Short Answer Buyers Need First

Run this five-point screen before asking for a certificate file or adding a certificate clause to a purchase order:

Buyer questionWhat it controls
Which site does the certificate cover?Certificates are site-specific and cannot be transferred between sites.
Which standard and version are named?ISO 22000:2018, FSSC 22000 scheme versions, and older editions differ.
What scope wording and categories appear?Scope must match PS production, not only storage, trading, or other lines.
When does it expire, and is it still valid?Validity, surveillance status, and suspension change over time.
Can the issuing body or a scheme register confirm it?Genuine certificates can usually be confirmed by the issuer or a register.

Do not reverse this sequence: a valid certificate for the wrong site or product line is still a genuine document, but it does not cover the ingredient you plan to buy.

As of August 2026, the EU baseline is Regulation (EC) 852/2004, which requires food business operators to operate HACCP-based procedures, and Commission Notice 2022/C 355/01, which explains how those procedures fit inside a food safety management system. Voluntary certificates such as ISO 22000:2018 and FSSC 22000 build on the same logic.

Why Certificate Review Is Its Own Qualification Step

PS supplier qualification usually involves several document layers: business registration, production licences, religious certification such as Halal or Kosher, specifications and COA, test reports, and audit findings. A food-safety management-system certificate is a different layer: it says an independent certification body audited the site's management system against a published standard. It does not say that every batch ever produced met every specification.

Keep the certificate review separate from:

  • the manufacturer audit, which examines actual production, cleaning, and control evidence rather than only certificate paperwork;
  • the food production licence review, which checks the government-issued licence and its product scope;
  • the Halal and Kosher reviews, which check religious-scheme certification and its product scope; and
  • third-party testing, which verifies laboratory data on a specific sample rather than a management system.

The certificate is one input into a decision, not the decision itself: a buyer can approve an uncertified supplier when audits, testing, and document controls are adequate, and a certified supplier still needs batch-level COA and specification evidence.

The Legal Baseline: HACCP-Based Procedures in the EU

Regulation (EC) 852/2004 and the HACCP obligation

Article 5 of Regulation (EC) 852/2004 requires food business operators to put in place, implement, and maintain permanent procedures based on the HACCP principles: hazard analysis, critical control points, critical limits, monitoring, corrective actions, verification, and documentation. The Regulation allows flexibility for certain small businesses; the Commission's 2022 Notice explains that good hygiene practices can be sufficient where no critical control points can be identified.

Regulation (EU) 2021/382 later amended the annexes of 852/2004 to add allergen management and food safety culture requirements. The Commission Notice clarifies how prerequisite programmes, HACCP-based procedures, and international standards such as ISO 22000 and the Codex General Principles of Food Hygiene fit together.

The practical implication: when a European importer accepts an ingredient, the buyer's own system should explain how supplier controls fit the finished-product hazard analysis. A supplier certificate supports that file; it does not transfer the buyer's obligations.

What this means for a non-EU PS supplier

EU food law applies to food business operators placing food on the Union market, normally the importer or first recipient. A non-EU manufacturer is not an EU food business operator, but the European buyer can contractually require equivalent evidence: a HACCP-based hazard analysis summary, prerequisite programme records, allergen-control statements, and any management-system certificate the supplier holds.

This is why "HACCP certified" is imprecise in Europe. HACCP is a set of principles embedded in EU law and international standards; there is no single EU "HACCP certificate" issued by a public authority. When a supplier claims HACCP certification, ask which scheme or audit produced the certificate, who issued it, and what it covers. A claim without a verifiable issuing body is not a certificate.

The Voluntary Certificates European Buyers Actually See

ISO 22000:2018

ISO 22000 is an international standard for food safety management systems. The current edition, ISO 22000:2018, applies to any organisation in the food chain and combines systems management, prerequisite programmes, and the HACCP principles; it replaced ISO 22000:2005.

For a PS buyer, the certificate should name the standard edition, the audited site, and the certification body. ISO 22000 certification is voluntary under EU food law but common in ingredient supply because European and North American customers ask for it.

FSSC 22000

FSSC 22000 is a certification scheme built on ISO 22000 plus sector-specific prerequisite programme and additional requirements, recognised by the Global Food Safety Initiative (GFSI) for food manufacturing scopes. The scheme owner publishes versioned requirements; Version 7 was published in May 2026, so confirm the scheme version and transition status on the certificate.

FSSC 22000 certificates include a defined scope with food-chain categories, governed by the scheme's annex on scope statements. The scheme also publishes a Certified Organization Register that lets buyers check certificate validity by organisation name.

Other GFSI-recognised schemes and ISO 9001

GFSI is a benchmarking initiative, not a certification body. It recognises programmes such as FSSC 22000, BRCGS, IFS, and SQF against its Benchmarking Requirements for specific scopes and versions. There is no "GFSI certificate": recognition applies to the scheme and version, not the individual certificate.

ISO 9001 is a quality management standard, not a food-safety standard. It can support process consistency, but it does not cover HACCP-based food-safety control, allergen management, or hygiene requirements. Treat it as quality evidence, separate from food-safety certification.

What Certificate Scope Means for a PS Ingredient

Scope is the most important sentence on any certificate and the most commonly skipped. Read it as a contract between the certification body and the site: it defines what was audited and therefore what the certificate supports.

Scope elementWhat to checkWhy it matters for PS
Site name and addressMatch the entity and factory address on quotation and licenceA group certificate does not cover another site
Standard and editionISO 22000:2018, FSSC 22000 version, or other schemeEditions and versions change what was audited
Food-chain categoriesConfirm categories include ingredient manufacturingCategory wording defines the audited activities
Product categoriesCheck for phospholipid or nutrition-ingredient wordingThe scope must include PS as produced
ExclusionsNote any exclusions or "not covered" linesExcluded activities fall outside the certificate
Issue and expiry datesConfirm the certificate is currentCertificates lapse and change between audits
Certification bodyNote the issuer and its accreditationThe issuer or an official register confirms authenticity

Two PS-specific examples: a genuine certificate whose scope says "storage and distribution" does not cover soy or sunflower PS production steps, and a certificate issued to a trading company without a production site does not cover a manufacturer's 25 kg drum line.

How to Verify a PS Supplier Certificate

Verification has four practical levels; use as many as the situation requires:

  1. Check the scheme register. For FSSC 22000, use the Certified Organization Register published by the scheme owner and search the organisation name. The register confirms validity against the scheme's records.
  2. Check accreditation databases. The IAF CertSearch database indexes certificates issued by certification bodies accredited under the IAF Multilateral Agreement. Not every certificate is listed, so a missing entry is a reason to ask, not proof of fraud.
  3. Ask the certification body. A written request confirming certificate number, site, scope, and validity is a normal audit step; genuine suppliers expect it.
  4. Review the audit trail. Request the current certificate, the last audit summary, nonconformities and corrective actions, and surveillance status, and compare them to the product and batch you are buying.

These checks belong in the supplier onboarding file and repeat at each annual supplier review, because certificates expire and scopes change.

Keep the certificate file linked to the COA and specification review: the certificate describes the system, the COA describes the batch, and European buyers need both.

Request Current Certificate Files for the Review

When a supplier is shortlisted, ask for one controlled pack instead of screenshots: current certificate with full scope wording, certificate number and validity dates, certification body contact, scheme register confirmation where available, audit summary, and the current specification and COA for the quoted grade. Send the request in writing so the file has a date and an owner. Common Buyer Mistakes

  1. Accepting a certificate logo in a quotation email without the actual document.
  2. Treating ISO 9001 as a food-safety certificate.
  3. Ignoring the scope sentence and assuming a "group" certificate covers every site.
  4. Comparing certificates from different schemes as if the scopes were identical.
  5. Checking expiry once at approval and never again at reorder.
  6. Using a certificate as a substitute for batch COA and specification review.
  7. Asking for "HACCP certification" without defining the scheme, version, issuing body, and scope.

FAQ

Which food-safety certificates should a European buyer request from a PS supplier?

Request the supplier's current food-safety management-system certificates, such as ISO 22000:2018 or a GFSI-recognised scheme like FSSC 22000, together with full scope wording, validity dates, certification body contact, and audit summary. Verify this layer separately from licences, religious certificates, specifications, and batch COA.

Is ISO 22000 certification required to sell food ingredients in the EU?

No. EU food law does not require non-EU ingredient suppliers to hold ISO 22000. EU hygiene rules require food business operators to apply HACCP-based procedures, and voluntary certificates are evidence supporting those controls. A buyer can contractually require a certificate or equivalent audit evidence.

Does a certificate prove that every PS batch is safe?

No. A certificate covers the site's management system at audit time. Batch safety is checked through specifications, COA, testing, and lot-linked controls; keep the certificate and the batch COA current and treat them as separate evidence.

How do buyers verify an FSSC 22000 or ISO 22000 certificate?

For FSSC 22000, search the scheme owner's Certified Organization Register by organisation name. For accredited ISO 22000 certificates, check IAF CertSearch, and for any certificate, contact the issuing body to confirm number, site, scope, and validity. Compare the scope to the exact product being purchased.

What does certificate scope cover for an ingredient manufacturer?

Scope names the audited site, standard and edition, food-chain and product categories, exclusions, and validity. For PS, it must cover ingredient manufacturing at the exact site and include the quoted grade; a genuine certificate with the wrong scope does not support the purchase.

Conclusion

A certificate is a snapshot of a management system, not a guarantee of a batch. For European PS buyers the disciplined workflow is short: identify the site, standard version, scope, validity, and issuing body; verify through the scheme register, accreditation database, or certification body; connect the certificate to the current specification and COA for the exact grade; and repeat the check at every annual review and reorder.

The same discipline applies to every document layer in a PS qualification file. Government licences, religious certificates, audit reports, specifications, COA, and test results each answer different questions; mixing them into one "compliance" statement creates the gaps that certificate review is meant to close.

For soy or sunflower PS procurement, keep certificate evidence separate from source selection and product documents. Confirm the exact source route, target assay, site, and destination market before comparing files, and date every review so scope and validity are never assumed.
