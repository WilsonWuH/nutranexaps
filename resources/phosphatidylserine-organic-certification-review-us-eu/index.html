Phosphatidylserine Organic Certification Review: What US and EU Buyers Should Verify

How US and EU buyers review organic phosphatidylserine claims: USDA NOP and EU 2018/848 rules, certificate checks, and proof documents.

Published August 12, 2026

View PS ProductsRequest Specifications

Phosphatidylserine (PS) is a processed, lecithin-derived ingredient, so its organic status is never a COA row: it is a certification claim proven with certificates, scope, and lot linkage. The direct answer for a buyer is to treat "organic phosphatidylserine" as a separate document review: decide first whether the finished product needs an organic claim; confirm which organic program applies in the destination market (the USDA National Organic Program in the US, Regulation (EU) 2018/848 in the EU); request the organic certificate covering the exact PS product, production site, and standards; check scope, certifier, validity, and restrictions; and only then accept the claim in a quotation or on a label. A supplier page that says "organic" is marketing, not evidence; the evidence is a current, in-scope certificate supported by specification, COA, and traceability documents tied to the batch.

This guide explains why organic review is separate from routine quality review, what an organic PS claim can mean, how US and EU rules treat PS and lecithin, and what a certificate must show. It is operational guidance, not legal or certification advice; confirm decisions with the accredited certifier or regulatory consultant for the market.

Why Organic Review Is a Separate Buyer Task

Most PS quality work asks whether a batch meets its specification: assay, moisture, peroxide value, heavy metals, microbiology, and similar parameters. Organic review asks a different question: is the ingredient certified under an organic program, and does that certification cover the quoted product, site, and production method? A batch can meet every quality specification and still have no organic status.

There are three practical reasons organic review belongs to the buyer:

  • The claim has legal consequences. In both markets, using an organic term on a label when the product is not certified is a compliance and misbranding risk, not a wording preference.
  • The certificate is the product. Organic status travels with a certificate naming the operation, product scope, standards, and validity. Without it, there is no organic product.
  • The chain matters. PS is produced from lecithin, which is produced from soy or sunflower seeds. Organic status must trace from the agricultural source through the lecithin processor to the PS processor; a gap anywhere breaks the claim.

The review also has a commercial edge: listings for "organic phosphatidylserine" exist, and some suppliers use the term loosely. A disciplined certificate review prevents paying an organic premium for a claim that cannot be documented.

What "Organic Phosphatidylserine" Can Legitimately Mean

Organic certification describes how agricultural products are produced and handled under a specific program. For a processed ingredient like PS, the claim is meaningful only when the whole chain is certified: the soy or sunflower seeds are grown under the program's rules, the lecithin is produced by a certified operation, and the PS conversion and finishing steps are covered by a certified operation whose scope includes the product.

In practice, buyers encounter three patterns:

Market patternWhat it usually meansWhat the buyer should do
"Organic PS" with no certificateA source-based marketing claim with no certification evidenceTreat as unverified; request the certificate before any premium or label commitment
PS from certified organic lecithin, PS itself not certifiedThe lecithin raw material is organic, but the PS processing operation is not coveredConfirm whether the claim is supportable; usually not a full organic claim
PS certified organic by an accredited certifierThe certificate names the PS product, site, standards, and validityVerify scope, accreditation, restrictions, and lot linkage, then file the certificate

The third pattern is the only one that supports a full organic label claim. Some brands market PS derived from certified organic sunflower lecithin, which is possible when the lecithin source is certified and the processing chain is covered. For a bulk buyer, the claim must be verified at the ingredient level, not copied from a retail label.

US Rules: NOP Certification, Composition, and the National List

In the United States, the term "organic" on food and dietary supplement labels is governed by the USDA National Organic Program (NOP) under 7 CFR Part 205. FDA does not define "organic"; it expects products carrying organic claims to comply with both NOP rules and FDA labeling rules.

Certification is required before the word "organic" is used

Under 7 CFR 205.100, an operation that produces or handles agricultural products sold, labeled, or represented as "100 percent organic", "organic", or "made with organic" must be certified by an accredited certifying agent. A supplier selling "organic PS" should therefore hold current NOP certification naming the product in scope.

Composition thresholds decide which claim is possible

7 CFR 205.301 defines the labeling categories for processed products:

Label categoryOrganic ingredient requirement (by weight, excluding water and salt)
"100 percent organic"100% organically produced ingredients
"organic"Not less than 95% organic ingredients; the remainder must be organic unless not commercially available, or must be nonagricultural or nonorganically produced agricultural products consistent with the National List
"made with organic (specified ingredients or food group(s))"At least 70% organic ingredients; the named organic ingredients may be identified on the label
Under 70%Organic ingredients may be represented only in the ingredient statement (7 CFR 205.305)

For a PS supplement, two points matter. A product labeled "organic" must be at least 95% organic by weight, and any non-organic agricultural ingredient must be allowed by the National List. PS is not on the list of nonorganically produced agricultural products allowed in products labeled "organic" under 7 CFR 205.606; the list does include "lecithin - de-oiled", but only when the product is not commercially available in organic form, a condition affirmed in the NOP final judgment in Harvey v. Johanns. In practical terms, non-organic PS cannot sit inside a product labeled "organic"; either the PS is certified, or the product needs a different claim structure such as "made with organic".

What a US organic label must show

Under 7 CFR 205.303, a packaged product labeled "organic" must identify each organic ingredient in the ingredient statement, show the percentage of organic ingredients where applicable, and identify the certifying agent of the finished-product handler. The USDA seal may be used only by certified operations, so the label requires certifier information the PS certificate must support.

The practical US review is document-based: confirm the PS route holds current NOP certification with the product in scope, check the National List position of any non-organic components, and match the claim category to the formula's organic content.

EU Rules: Regulation (EU) 2018/848 and the Authorised Lists

In the European Union, organic production and labelling are governed by Regulation (EU) 2018/848, which applies to food, including food supplements. The framework is similar in discipline to the US one: a claim requires certification, and non-organic agricultural ingredients are allowed only under strict conditions.

The 95% rule for organic terms

Under Article 30 of Regulation (EU) 2018/848, organic terms may be used in the sales description of a processed food only when at least 95% of the agricultural ingredients by weight are organic; below that, organic terms may appear only in the ingredient list. For PS, the consequence is direct: non-certified PS blocks an "organic" sales description, because it counts against the 95% threshold and is not on the authorized non-organic list.

Non-organic agricultural ingredients require authorization

Article 24 of Regulation (EU) 2018/848 allows the Commission to authorize products and substances for organic production and to keep them in restrictive lists. Annex II, Part IV states that only authorized food additives, processing aids, and non-organic agricultural ingredients may be used in processed organic food; the lists sit in Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2021/1165. PS is not on the authorized non-organic agricultural ingredient list, so the robust path for an organic PS claim is certification of the PS itself.

Lecithin in the EU organic lists

Lecithins (E 322) appear in the EU lists as a food additive permitted in organic production. Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2023/2229, which amended the lists, moved lecithins to an "only from organic production" requirement. That change concerns lecithin as an additive, but it signals that EU organic sourcing for lecithin-derived materials is tightening. The EU organic production logo is also compulsory on packaged products using organic terms, subject to the conditions in the Regulation, so artwork depends on the certificate chain.

For an EU buyer, the review is: confirm the PS product holds current certification recognized under Regulation (EU) 2018/848 with the product in scope, verify the 95% calculation for the formula, and check that any lecithin-related additive use follows the authorized list.

What the Organic Certificate Must Show

An organic certificate is a controlled document. Verify these fields on any certificate offered for PS:

Certificate fieldWhat the buyer should verify
Certifying agentAn accredited certifier matching the program claimed (USDA NOP, EU recognized control body)
Certificate numberA traceable identifier that can be checked with the certifier
Operation and siteThe named producer must be the PS or lecithin manufacturer at the quoted address
Product scopePS (or the lecithin base) by name and grade, not a generic line
StandardsThe program and standard referenced, such as NOP or Regulation (EU) 2018/848
ValidityIssue and expiry dates; current for the quotation and shipment
RestrictionsAny conditions, exclusions, or transitional limitations on scope
Lot linkageWhether the certificate or COA connects certified status to the exact batch

Two checks are often missed. The certificate must cover the operation that actually makes the product: a certificate held by a trading company does not certify a factory's output. And the scope must name the product: a certificate for "lecithin" does not cover "phosphatidylserine".

Building an Organic Evidence File

Create one controlled file per product route and review cycle, alongside the documents for PS ingredients baseline:

  1. State the claim category you are targeting (US "organic" vs "made with organic"; EU sales description vs ingredient-list reference).
  2. Request the current organic certificate for the exact PS product and production site.
  3. Verify certifier, certificate number, scope, standards, validity, and restrictions against the program rules.
  4. Confirm the source route (soy or sunflower) matches the certificate, and cross-check with the soy vs sunflower PS comparison.
  5. Tie the certificate to the batch with the current COA and lot traceability records.
  6. Record the decision and evidence in the supplier onboarding and supplier quality agreement files.
  7. Confirm the formula and label with the certifier or regulatory consultant before artwork.

Keep the file with the same discipline as the COA and specification review: current documents, exact product names, and visible batch linkage.

Need current PS specifications, COA samples, and source evidence to start the review? Request the specification and document evidence with your source preference, target assay, application, destination market, and any organic requirement you are evaluating.

When Organic Is Not Available: What to Do Instead

Most commercial PS is not certified organic, and a buyer does not always need it to be: certification matters only when the finished product carries an organic claim. Practical alternatives:

  • Confirm the claim is actually needed. Many products do not use organic terms; documented conventional PS with specification, COA, and source evidence is the standard route.
  • Use a lower claim category. In the US, "made with organic" or an ingredient-statement reference may be possible when other formula ingredients are organic. In the EU, organic terms can appear only in the ingredient list under Article 30(2).
  • Qualify the source for a future change. If an organic launch is planned, ask whether organic-certified lecithin or organic PS could be supplied, and with what certificate and timeline.
  • Document the decision. Record why organic was or was not required so the finished-product file and label stay consistent.

This is also the honest supplier conversation. Nutranexa supplies soy PS and sunflower PS routes with documented specifications and COA evidence, and does not claim organic certification for those products. Buyers evaluating an organic claim should verify certification at the source level; others can proceed on the documented conventional route.

Supplier Questions to Ask Before Ordering

Add these to the quotation and qualification workflow:

  1. Does the quoted PS product hold current organic certification under the USDA NOP, Regulation (EU) 2018/848, or an equivalent recognized program?
  2. Which certifier issued it, what is the certificate number, and does the scope name this product and site?
  3. Is the certified status tied to the soy or sunflower lecithin source, and can the certificate chain be provided?
  4. What validity, restrictions, or transitional conditions affect the quoted grade?
  5. Can the certificate be linked to the exact batch on the COA and packing list for each shipment?
  6. If not certified, can organic-certified lecithin or organic PS be supplied, and with what documentation and timeline?

Record the answers in the supplier questionnaire and keep the certificates in the distributor document pack or onboarding file; certifiers and customers will request the same evidence.

Conclusion

Organic phosphatidylserine is a certification claim, not a quality parameter. The review starts with whether the finished product needs an organic claim, then verifies the destination-market program: USDA NOP certification and the National List in the US, Regulation (EU) 2018/848 and the authorized lists in the EU. The certificate must name the exact product, site, standards, certifier, and validity, and tie to the quoted batch. Non-certified PS cannot be added to a product labeled "organic", because it is not an authorized non-organic agricultural ingredient in either market. A disciplined certificate review protects the label, the premium, and the supplier relationship.

FAQ

Is phosphatidylserine organic?

Phosphatidylserine is organic only if the PS production chain is certified under a program such as the USDA NOP or Regulation (EU) 2018/848. Most commercial PS is not certified, so verify any claim with a current certificate naming the product, site, and standards.

Can a supplement containing phosphatidylserine be labeled organic in the US?

Only if it meets the NOP category rules in 7 CFR 205.301. A product labeled "organic" must be at least 95% organic by weight, and PS is not on the National List of nonorganically produced agricultural products allowed in organic-labeled products, so the PS would need certification or a different claim category.

Can a supplement containing phosphatidylserine be labeled organic in the EU?

Under Regulation (EU) 2018/848, organic terms in the sales description require at least 95% of agricultural ingredients by weight to be organic, and non-organic agricultural ingredients may be used only if authorized. PS is not on the authorized list, so it would need certification.

What does "organic phosphatidylserine" mean on an ingredient listing?

It should mean the PS is certified organic by an accredited certifier, with product, site, and standards in scope. Without a certificate, the term is only a marketing claim; request the certificate before paying a premium.

Is lecithin allowed as an ingredient in organic products?

In the US, de-oiled lecithin is on the National List in 7 CFR 205.606, allowed in products labeled "organic" only when organic lecithin is not commercially available. In the EU, lecithins (E 322) are on the authorized additive list, amended in 2023 to require lecithins from organic production.

What documents prove an organic PS claim?

The organic certificate showing certifier, certificate number, operation and site, product scope, standards, validity, and restrictions, plus the raw-material certificate chain, current COA, and lot traceability records.
