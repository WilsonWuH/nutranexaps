Retest Periods on Phosphatidylserine COAs: US/EU Buyer Guide

How US and EU buyers handle retest periods on phosphatidylserine COAs: definitions, re-qualification testing, extension data, and contract language.

Published October 6, 2026

View PS ProductsRequest Specifications

A lot lands at your warehouse, the drum looks fine, and then your QA reviewer flags one line on the Certificate of Analysis: "Retest date: 2028-03." Not an expiry date — a retest date. If your specification sheet says "shelf life: 24 months" and your supplier's COA says "retest: 36 months, subject to re-qualification," you have a documentation mismatch that will surface in your next customer audit. Retest periods are standard practice in the phospholipid industry, but most buyers have never written a procedure for them. This article explains what a retest period actually commits a supplier to, what data should sit behind it, how to re-qualify an aging lot of phosphatidylserine powder, and how to write the term into your specification and quality agreement so the decision path is clear before the material is 30 months old. Why PS Suppliers Use Retest Periods Instead of Expiry Dates

Phosphatidylserine powder is a phospholipid ingredient: hygroscopic, oxidation-sensitive, and typically 50–70% PS content by assay, with the balance being other phospholipids and a carrier such as soy lecithin or a sunflower-derived fraction. Properly packaged — nitrogen-flushed drums with barrier liners — the chemistry degrades slowly, and suppliers hesitate to certify a fixed expiry date that would force them to write off inventory and recall stock from distributors on a fixed calendar.

Instead, most PS manufacturers assign a retest period of 24 to 36 months. The claim means: "Based on the stability data we hold, this material is expected to meet specification until the retest date, provided storage conditions are maintained; after that date, the material must be tested before use." This convention, borrowed from pharmaceutical excipient practice, transfers the end-of-period decision to the holder of the material — which, after you take delivery, is you.

The regulatory framing differs slightly across your two markets. In the US, 21 CFR Part 111 requires dietary supplement manufacturers to verify that components meet specifications and to establish a shelf life for the finished product, but it does not prescribe how an ingredient supplier expresses dating on the ingredient COA. In the EU, Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011 requires a "date of minimum durability" on the finished food, justified on your own data — an ingredient retest period is an input to that justification, not a substitute for it. Either way, your finished-product claim cannot simply inherit the ingredient's retest period without documented reasoning.

Retest Period vs Shelf Life vs Expiry: Definitions for Your Spec Sheet

Use these three terms precisely, because auditors will ask:

  • Expiry date: after this date the material must not be used, full stop.
  • Shelf life: the period during which the material is expected to remain within specification when stored as directed; often used interchangeably with expiry in US ingredient trade.
  • Retest period: the period after which the material may still be used if it passes re-qualification testing against the full release specification.

Your raw material specification sheet should state which of the three applies, the length of the period, and the required storage conditions ("below 25 °C, relative humidity below 60%, unopened original packaging"). If the supplier declares 36 months to retest, your specification should mirror that and reference the re-qualification procedure by name. A mismatch between your spec sheet and the supplier's COA — a very common finding — is easily avoided at vendor qualification, and painful to fix after three years of purchase orders.

What a Retest Claim Must Be Backed By

A retest period is only as good as the stability program behind it. When you qualify a PS supplier, request a stability data summary, not just the claim. What you want to see:

  • Real-time stability protocol: at least two production-scale lots, tested at 0, 6, 12, 18, 24, and 36 months at label storage conditions.
  • Trended attributes that actually drive PS degradation: peroxide value (typical acceptance ≤5 meq/kg, tighter for premium grades), anisidine value where offered, moisture/water activity, assay (enzymatic or LC method), color, and microbiology.
  • Accelerated data (e.g., 40 °C/75% RH for 6 months) used as supporting, not primary, evidence.
  • Statement of which lots the claim covers — a claim generated on one pilot lot does not support 36 months on production material.

If a supplier claims 36 months to retest but can show only 12 months of real-time data plus extrapolation, treat the claim as 12 months of demonstrated data with a risk judgment attached — and shorten your own internal use-by accordingly. This is exactly the kind of gap that a COA red-flags review should catch at qualification, before your formulation is locked to that material.

Re-qualifying Material Approaching the Retest Date

When a lot is 60–90 days from its retest date, your QA team should trigger the re-qualification procedure defined in your specification. A practical re-qualification panel for PS powder:

Practical logistics: pull a composite sample per your sampling plan, ship to a third-party lab, and budget roughly USD 800–1,500 and 2–4 weeks for the panel. Cost allocation should be settled in the quality agreement: if the supplier's stability program supports the retest claim, the buyer typically pays for re-qualification; if the material fails and the claim was unsupported, the supplier reimburses and replaces.

Two dispositions matter. If the material passes, document a re-qualification record with a new internal use-by date — commonly one further year, then a second retest before mandatory rejection, unless the supplier's data supports longer. If it fails on oxidative attributes, do not negotiate: rancid PS will carry through to finished product sensory defects and consumer complaints.

Extending a Retest Period: The Data Package

Sometimes you hold a large lot and want more than one re-qualification cycle. The correct route is a formal extension from the supplier, backed by their stability data on that lot code or an equivalent production lot, not a unilateral extension on your side. Request: the lot-specific stability results, the extended retest date in writing (a controlled specification revision or COA amendment, not an email), and a change-control record if the extension crosses into a new packaging configuration. Your change-notification procedure should list "retest period extension" as a notification trigger.

Contract and Specification Language Checklist

Write these points into the raw material specification and the supplier quality agreement at onboarding:

  • Declared retest period (months) and storage conditions, identical on spec sheet and COA.
  • Stability data summary available at qualification and on request at each extension.
  • Re-qualification test panel, sampling basis, and pass/fail disposition rules.
  • Cost allocation for re-qualification testing in pass and fail scenarios.
  • Mandatory supplier notification for retest extensions, claim reductions, or stability program changes.
  • Reserve sample availability for at least the retest period plus one year.

Buyers who write this language early almost never face a dead lot; buyers who rely on the supplier's generic spec sheet frequently do.

FAQ

Q: Can I use phosphatidylserine powder after the retest date on the COA?

A: Only after documented re-qualification against the full release specification. If the material passes the panel — assay, peroxide value, moisture, color, odor, microbiology — record the new internal use-by date. If testing cannot be completed before you need the material, plan ahead: retest cycles typically take 2–4 weeks.

Q: Is a retest period acceptable instead of an expiry date for a US dietary ingredient?

A: Yes. FDA regulation under 21 CFR Part 111 does not dictate the dating convention on an ingredient COA; it requires you to verify component specifications and establish your finished product's shelf life. What matters is that your specification acknowledges the retest concept and your procedure defines re-qualification.

Q: How does a retest period affect my EU minimum durability date?

A: The ingredient retest period is an input, not an answer. Your finished product's durability date under Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011 must be justified by your own finished-product stability data, which should start from the freshest ingredient date you can reasonably procure.

Q: Who pays for re-qualification testing?

A: Whatever your quality agreement says. The market norm: buyer pays when the material passes and the supplier's claim was properly supported; supplier reimburses testing and replacement costs when the material fails against a claim its stability data did not support.

Q: How many times can a lot be re-qualified?

A: There is no universal rule. Many buyers allow one re-qualification cycle of 12 months and require rejection after a second failed or uneconomic cycle. Multiple retests of oxidatively sensitive phospholipids are rarely worth the risk to finished-product sensory quality.

Conclusion

A retest date on a phosphatidylserine COA is not a loophole and not a liability — it is a defined decision point. Treat it like any other specification parameter: confirm the stability data behind the claim at vendor qualification, mirror the term in your own spec sheet, define the re-qualification panel and disposition rules before you need them, and settle cost allocation in the quality agreement. Buyers who manage retest periods procedurally extend usable inventory safely and pass audits cleanly; buyers who ignore the date write off stock or ship oxidative defects to their customers.
