FSMA 204 and Phosphatidylserine Imports: A Traceability Compliance Guide for US Buyers

How the FDA Food Traceability Rule (FSMA 204) applies to imported phosphatidylserine — FTL screening, Traceability Lot Codes, Key Data Elements, and.

Published September 24, 2026

View PS ProductsRequest Specifications

The FDA's Food Traceability Final Rule — FSMA Section 204, codified at 21 CFR Part 1, Subpart S — adds recordkeeping obligations for foods on the Food Traceability List and foods containing them as ingredients. Its compliance date is 20 January 2028, now inside the qualification horizon of every US buyer sourcing phosphatidylserine for the American market.

For an imported ingredient, the rule reaches two parties at once: the importer holding the ingredient in the United States, and the foreign manufacturer whose lot-level records make the importer's data complete. A buyer who waits until 2027 to raise this with an overseas supplier will find that documentation redesign takes longer than the calendar allows. This article sets out what the rule demands and what to request from suppliers now.

What FSMA 204 Actually Requires

The rule obliges persons who manufacture, process, pack, or hold listed foods to maintain records linking each lot through the supply chain. Four concepts carry the weight.

The Food Traceability List (FTL) identifies foods in scope, including foods using listed items as ingredients. The Traceability Lot Code (TLC) is the unique identifier assigned to a quantity of food where the rule requires it; it must appear in every downstream record. Key Data Elements (KDEs) — quantities, dates, locations, business names, the TLC, and reference record types — must be captured at each Critical Tracking Event (CTE): receiving, transforming, creating, shipping, and holding. Every subject entity must also maintain a written traceability plan and produce required information to the FDA within 24 hours of a request during an outbreak or recall investigation.

Where Phosphatidylserine Stands on the Food Traceability List

The first step is a documented screening: is your specific PS ingredient, as manufactured and as entered into your finished product, in scope? The FTL is a defined list, not a category judgment, and the answer depends on classification.

Three positions are common. If the ingredient or product is clearly on the list, full KDE and CTE recordkeeping applies. If it falls outside, the rule does not attach — but commercial obligations often still do, because brand customers increasingly require FTL-style documentation contractually. Borderline cases deserve a written rationale in the compliance file, not an informal decision in an email thread.

Traceability Lot Codes and Key Data Elements in an Import Chain

An imported PS supply chain typically runs: lecithin sourcing and enzymatic conversion at the manufacturer, drying and milling, quality release, packing, ocean freight, arrival at the US importer or contract manufacturer, transformation into finished form, and distribution. Each link is a CTE with its own KDEs.

Two practical questions dominate. Who establishes the TLC — the manufacturer at packing, or the importer at receiving? Best practice favours the earliest practical establishment at the manufacturing site, where the lot is physically and analytically defined. And does the TLC travel consistently? Every document travelling with the drum — invoice, bill of lading, packing list, certificate of analysis — should carry the same TLC string, because any mismatch between shipping and quality documents breaks the chain during an investigation.

Supplier Collaboration: What to Demand Before January 2028

An importer cannot build traceability records from a supplier's invoice alone. The documentation set to request from a PS manufacturer is specific and finite: a written statement of the supplier's traceability structure — how production lots are coded, whether one drum equals one TLC or a campaign lot spans many, and how sub-lots link to parent lots; sample completed records for one recent lot across all CTEs, showing actual data fields rather than promises; a copy of the supplier's own traceability procedures, because the data must originate there; agreement on document carriage, meaning the TLC printed on the COA, packing list, and bill of lading and transmitted electronically at shipment release; and a notification protocol for post-shipment events that change a lot's identity.

Suppliers with electronic batch documentation can meet this with modest changes; paper-only suppliers may need quarters, not weeks.

Your Traceability Plan and the Road to 20 January 2028

The traceability plan is the artefact the FDA inspects. It must describe how you assign TLCs where you are the establisher, the records you keep for each CTE, how finished product lots link to ingredient TLCs, the contact responsible for FDA requests, and how you will provide electronic sortable spreadsheet output when required.

A plan never exercised is a liability. Run a mock trace: pick one recent finished batch and reconstruct every ingredient lot behind it — including the PS — within a working day. The exercise reliably exposes gaps: a repack without a recorded TLC, a supplier invoice missing the lot code, a co-packer's internal codes with no linkage to yours.

A realistic sequence to the deadline: complete the FTL screening; issue the supplier documentation request; agree TLC establishment and document carriage; align ERP fields to capture KDEs at each internal CTE; draft the traceability plan; run the first mock trace and remediate; then move into routine operation well before the date.

FAQ

Q: Is phosphatidylserine itself on the Food Traceability List?

A: It depends on the listed forms and how your ingredient and product categories are classified. Screen against the current FTL text and document the rationale. Even where an ingredient falls outside the list, customers often require equivalent documentation contractually.

Q: Who should establish the Traceability Lot Code for an imported ingredient?

A: The earliest practical point — usually the manufacturer at packing — gives the cleanest chain, because the TLC then matches the lot definition used for quality release and travels on every document through the port into your warehouse.

Q: Do we need electronic records?

A: Records may be paper, but must be produced legibly within 24 hours of an FDA request, and an electronic sortable spreadsheet may be requested. Records linked electronically at data entry are the only structure that meets that without improvisation.

Conclusion

FSMA 204 turns traceability from a quality habit into a legal record set with a deadline, and for imported phosphatidylserine the decisive work sits between importer and manufacturer: who assigns the lot code, which documents carry it, and whether the data can be assembled within 24 hours of a request. Buyers who screen now and mock-trace before anyone forces a real one will pass 20 January 2028 calmly.
