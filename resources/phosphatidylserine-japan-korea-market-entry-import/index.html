Phosphatidylserine Japan & Korea Import Compliance Guide

How phosphatidylserine enters Japan and Korea: import notification, MFDS requirements, documents, and a pre-shipment checklist for B2B buyers.

Published September 14, 2026

View PS ProductsRequest Specifications

Japan and South Korea are sophisticated, high-value markets for phosphatidylserine, but they process ingredient imports through their own regulatory systems, in their own languages, on their own timelines. A PS shipment that clears EU or US customs without friction can still be held in Yokohama or Incheon if the importer's paperwork does not match what the local framework expects: the right food import notification in Japan, the correct ingredient pathway and declaration in Korea, and documentation that survives port-side review. This guide describes both frameworks at a working level, lists the documents to prepare before shipping, and shows where local verification matters most.

How Japan and Korea Treat Phosphatidylserine as an Ingredient

Both countries regulate phosphatidylserine through their food systems, but the pathway depends on how the finished product positions itself. In Japan, PS-derived ingredients are used in foods for specified health uses and in ordinary packaged foods, and each route carries different review expectations. In Korea, the same ingredient can move through the general food ingredient system or the health functional food system, and the two routes differ in permitted product types, function-related labeling, and the depth of ingredient review.

Two practical consequences follow for buyers:

  • The ingredient is only half the question: whether the finished product may carry a function-related message depends on the pathway, not just the raw material's status.
  • Status is verified locally: the importer of record and, ideally, a local regulatory consultant confirm the current status for the specific grade, source, and use before the first commercial order.

Because neither regulator publishes simple one-line answers for every ingredient grade, the safe working assumption for a B2B buyer is this: confirm pathway first, then build the document pack to match it.

Japan: The Food Sanitation Act and Import Notification

Japan's food import system operates under the Food Sanitation Act, administered by the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare (MHLW). Under this framework, imported food and food ingredients intended for sale in Japan must go through an import notification process at the quarantine station with jurisdiction over the port or airport of entry. Filing is generally handled by the importer of record, which is one reason your choice of Japanese importing partner matters as much as your choice of raw material supplier.

What buyers should understand about the process at a general level:

  • Notification precedes release: the importer submits prescribed forms describing product, producer, and use; authorities may then review documents or order laboratory testing before release.
  • Monitoring can be targeted: a phospholipid ingredient with clean documentation can still be sampled like any other food consignment.
  • Product definition must be consistent: the name, source, and processing in the notification should match the specification sheet, COA, and labels, since document mismatches are a common cause of port-side questions.

The importer of record owns the filing, but the supplier owns the accuracy of the underlying documents. Exporters should expect to provide manufacturing-process descriptions, composition statements, and test results on request, in forms the importer can attach to the notification.

Japan: Specifications, Standards, and Documentation Expectations

Alongside the Food Sanitation Act, Japan maintains the Specifications and Standards for Foods, Food Additives, etc. - the reference work that defines compositional and purity expectations for categories of food ingredients and additives. Where an ingredient falls within this framework, the importer must be able to show that the shipped product conforms to the applicable specifications, and port inspections may check against them.

A practical document pack for a PS shipment to Japan therefore includes:

  • A detailed specification sheet listing source, composition, physical properties, and contaminant limits.
  • A per-lot certificate of analysis from the manufacturer, using recognized methods.
  • A manufacturing-process summary sufficient for the importer to describe the product's origin and treatment.
  • Allergen-related information: soy-derived PS touches Japan's allergen labeling expectations for processed foods, and the importer will need source clarity to label finished products correctly.
  • Free-sale or origin evidence where the importer requests it for their own records.

None of these documents is exotic, but two details cause most friction. Translation: a specification sheet available only in English slows every review. And consistency: source, assay, and process must tell one coherent story across the spec, COA, and process summary - a Japan-ready pack avoids repeated round trips during the notification window.

Korea: The MFDS Framework for Ingredients and Health Functional Foods

In South Korea, the Ministry of Food and Drug Safety (MFDS) oversees both ordinary foods and health functional foods (HFF). For an ingredient buyer, the central planning question is which system the finished product will enter, because the ingredient requirements and the labeling possibilities differ between them.

At a general level:

  • General foods: ingredients are subject to MFDS food ingredient standards; material with established food use typically proceeds through import declaration under the general food system.
  • Health functional foods: HFF products follow a dedicated framework with its own ingredient and functional-content rules, and novel ingredient uses generally require review. If the product will carry a function-related claim, confirm the ingredient's HFF status early - that pathway has longer lead times and product-level review.
  • Ingredient standards: whichever route applies, the material should meet the relevant purity and contaminant expectations, evidenced by specifications and test results the importer holds.

The buyer's protection here is simple: ask the Korean counterparty, in writing, which system the product enters, and require their confirmation that the PS grade, source, and use fit that system before contract signing.

Korea: Import Declaration, Inspection, and Labeling Basics

Korean food imports proceed through an MFDS-administered import declaration and safety review process. The importer of record files a declaration for each consignment; depending on the product's history, category, and monitoring status, authorities may order document review, sensory or laboratory inspection, or sampling before release. Products with a clean import history generally move faster than new items, which is one more reason to plan the first shipment with generous time margins.

Finished-product considerations the raw material buyer should keep in view:

  • Labeling: Korean labeling rules apply to the finished product and are largely the importer's obligation, but the raw material documents must supply what labels depend on: true source, composition, and allergen information.
  • Korean-language documentation: as in Japan, declarations work best when the importer can translate and attach the manufacturer's documents without ambiguity.
  • Consistency across shipments: later lots should match the approved documentation exactly; any change in source, assay, or process reaches the importer before the next declaration.

Buyer Checklist Before Shipping to Japan or Korea

Before the first commercial PS shipment to either market, work through this list with your local partner:

  • [ ] Confirm the finished-product pathway (ordinary food vs functional product) in writing with the importer or distributor.
  • [ ] Confirm the ingredient's current status for that pathway with a licensed local import agent or regulatory consultant - do not rely solely on the supplier's general statement.
  • [ ] Agree the document pack: specification sheet, per-lot COA, manufacturing-process summary, allergen and source statements, and any origin or free-sale evidence requested.
  • [ ] Align product identity across all documents: name, botanical source, assay, and physical form must match everywhere.
  • [ ] Ask whether Japanese or Korean translations of key documents are needed, and who prepares them.
  • [ ] Plan the first-shipment timeline around possible document review or port-side testing, and keep extra stock cover until the first lots clear.
  • [ ] Set a change-notification rule: any change to source, grade, or process reaches the importer before the next shipment.

FAQ

Q: Can our company file the Japanese import notification ourselves?

As a foreign exporter, no - filing goes through the importer of record in Japan. Your job is accurate specifications, COAs, and process information the importer can use, which makes the importing partner part of your compliance strategy.

Q: Is phosphatidylserine treated as a health functional food ingredient in Korea?

It depends on the product and the ingredient's current MFDS status. General food and health functional food routes follow different rules, and function-related labeling is available only through the HFF system - confirm the pathway with your Korean partner or a local regulatory consultant before committing to a product concept.

Q: What documents should a PS supplier have ready for these markets?

A specification sheet aligned to the shipped grade, per-lot certificates of analysis, a clear manufacturing-process summary, source and allergen statements, and willingness to complete importer questionnaires. Consistency across documents matters as much as completeness.

Q: How much extra time should we allow for the first import?

Plan conservatively: a first consignment may face document review or port-side testing, and a new ingredient history takes time to establish. Buffer weeks on the first order protect your launch schedule.

Conclusion

Japan and Korea reward preparation. The frameworks differ - MHLW import notification under the Food Sanitation Act on one side, MFDS declaration with a separate health functional food track on the other - but the playbook is the same: identify the pathway early, confirm ingredient status through a licensed local partner, assemble a consistent, translation-ready document pack, and plan the first shipment with room for review. A complete, coherent document set turns a potentially slow first import into a routine one.
