Phosphatidylserine Vegan Statement Review for US and Canada Buyers

Learn how U.S. and Canadian buyers should review a phosphatidylserine vegan statement and separate ingredient qualification from finished-label use.

Published July 28, 2026

Посмотреть продукты PSЗапросить характеристики

Phosphatidylserine buyers in North America often solve the first sourcing questions early: source route, target assay, COA logic, MOQ, packaging, and customer document needs. Then a narrower commercialization question appears and stalls the project: can this phosphatidylserine really be supported as vegan, and what evidence is strong enough to say so in a U.S. or Canadian file?

The short answer is that a phosphatidylserine vegan statement is useful only when it is tied to the exact route, ingredient composition, and downstream use under review. FDA states that claims such as "vegan" or "non-GMO" do not require any premarket or postmarket submission to FDA, but like any other food or dietary-supplement labeling claim, they must be truthful and non-misleading. U.S. food misbranding law in 21 U.S.C. 343 and labeling rules in 21 CFR 101.4 и 21 CFR 101.36 also reinforce that ingredient identity and source disclosure still matter. In Canada, CFIA guidance treats vegetarian and vegan claims as composition and quality claims and states that claims about the absence of an ingredient or substance must be factual and not misleading. The practical implication is an inference from those primary sources: buyers should not treat a generic vegan note as a marketing accessory. They should treat it as a controlled evidence question.

The Short Answer Buyers Need First

If your team is reviewing whether a phosphatidylserine ingredient can sit behind a vegan statement, the cleanest working process is:

  1. Lock the exact PS route first: general PS, soy PS, or sunflower PS.
  2. Define the downstream use: supplier qualification only, customer questionnaire support, technical data pack, or finished-label review.
  3. Ask what the supplier's vegan file actually is: statement, specification language, questionnaire answer, or broader supporting packet.
  4. Compare the file to the route, supporting ingredient information, and any other ingredients that will appear in the finished product.
  5. Separate ingredient-level evidence from finished-product label permission.
  6. Close with a documented go, hold, revise, or escalate decision.

The operational point is simple: a vegan statement is strongest when it answers one defined buyer decision. If procurement, QA, regulatory, and sales are using the same word to mean four different things, the file looks complete while nobody can show what it really covers.

Why a Vegan Statement Is Its Own Buyer Task

U.S. rules focus on truthful labeling, not automatic claim permission

One of the easiest mistakes in ingredient commercialization is assuming that a voluntary claim is either fully unregulated or fully preapproved. FDA's dietary-supplement labeling guidance does not support either shortcut. The agency says a vegan claim does not require a premarket or postmarket submission, but it still must be truthful and non-misleading. 21 U.S.C. 343 frames false or misleading labeling as misbranding, while 21 CFR 101.36 says source ingredients in dietary supplements must be identified in accordance with 21 CFR 101.4.

That matters because a phosphatidylserine buyer often receives a statement file before the downstream label is stable. One team may be reviewing a bulk ingredient only. Another may already be designing a finished supplement label or marketplace listing. A supplier statement can help the first team and still be too narrow for the second.

For U.S.-facing supplement projects, the vegan question usually appears at four moments:

  • early ingredient screening
  • customer questionnaire or distributor technical-file support
  • private-label artwork review
  • contract-manufacturer intake before a finished formula is locked

Those are not the same decisions. A file that supports early sourcing may not be enough for finished-label approval.

Canadian buyers still need factual, substantiated claim control

Canadian buyers reach the same operational problem through a different compliance path. CFIA's guidance on food composition and quality claims places vegetarian and vegan claims inside the larger rule that claims must be factual and not misleading. CFIA's method-of-production claim guidance also says regulated parties should be able to substantiate claims during compliance and enforcement activities.

The business implication is similar to the U.S. position: Canada does not give a buyer a shortcut that says "plant-based source equals ready-to-use vegan claim forever." The buyer still needs substantiation tied to the exact product and the exact use of the claim. For phosphatidylserine projects, that means defining whether the file is being used for ingredient qualification, customer response, or finished commercial labeling in Canada.

This is why a vegan statement should not be buried inside a broad packaging declaration review, Supplement Facts review, or customer questionnaire control workflow. Those pages answer adjacent questions, but they do not replace vegan-claim substantiation.

A Six-Step Workflow for Reviewing a PS Vegan Statement

Lock the exact route and commercial stage first

Start with product identity, not the label copy. Record whether the route under review is general phosphatidylserine, soy phosphatidylserine, or sunflower phosphatidylserine. Record the supplier entity, product code, lot or commercial grade reference, intended country, and whether the file is needed for ingredient approval, distributor resale, or a finished supplement program.

This matters because the same company can sell multiple PS routes and buyers often compress them into one folder labeled "PS vegan file." That is weak document control. A better process ties the statement to one stable route and one named decision.

Identify what kind of file is actually in hand

The second step is to stop calling every commercial note "the vegan statement." In practice, buyers may receive:

  1. a signed supplier vegan statement
  2. a questionnaire answer saying the ingredient is vegan
  3. a specification or technical sheet with relevant route information
  4. a broader product-compliance packet that includes several voluntary claims

Each file type has a different evidentiary role. A questionnaire answer may help sales communicate with a distributor. A signed statement may support ingredient qualification. A route-specific specification may help confirm naming and source logic. None of those automatically authorize a finished-label claim if the downstream product includes other ingredients or packaging decisions outside the file's scope.

File in handWhat it can supportWhat it does not automatically prove
Supplier vegan statementIngredient-level claim support for one defined routeThat every finished product using the ingredient is vegan
Specification or technical sheetRoute identity and supporting composition logicThat marketing wording has already been cleared
Customer questionnaire answerA controlled response to one buyer inquiryA permanent approval for later routes or formulas
Finished-product mockupIntended downstream wordingThat the ingredient file and formula file still match

Check source and ingredient identity before claim wording

This is the point where 21 CFR 101.4 и 21 CFR 101.36 become practical rather than theoretical. The buyer should confirm that the route being described is the same route being sourced and that source ingredients are identified in a way the downstream label team can use correctly.

For phosphatidylserine, strong review questions include:

  • Does the file clearly identify the exact phosphatidylserine route being quoted?
  • Does the route stay stable across specification, COA path, sales communication, and customer questionnaire use?
  • Is the buyer reviewing a bulk ingredient route only, or a later finished-product claim that will depend on other ingredients too?

This is also where a vegan review separates itself from the site's existing allergen statement article. A soy-derived ingredient can still need vegan substantiation, and a vegan-support file does not remove the need for separate soy-allergen review where applicable.

Keep ingredient qualification separate from finished-label permission

The fourth step is the one commercial teams skip most often. Ingredient-level evidence and finished-label permission are not the same approval.

An ingredient statement may support a conclusion such as: "this quoted phosphatidylserine route is supported by the supplier as vegan." That is narrower than: "our final supplement may now carry a vegan claim." The finished product may include capsule shells, flavors, carriers, processing aids, or other ingredients outside the scope of the raw-material file. The article's point is not to speculate about any unverified Nutranexa input. The point is to keep claim scope disciplined.

That is why a vegan review belongs beside, not inside, gluten-free statement review, GMO statement review, and allergen statement review. Each claim answers a different downstream question.

Define how the statement may be reused

The fifth step is to decide whether the file may be reused and where. Good document control usually names:

  • the route it covers
  • the company name it covers
  • the downstream teams allowed to use it
  • the next trigger for re-review

Examples of re-review triggers include source-route change, a new finished formula, a new customer questionnaire, a new label project, or a document-version refresh. Without that control, one old statement gets copied across customer packs and private-label projects long after the route or wording context has changed.

If your team already maintains a controlled answer library, the vegan statement should sit there with version logic rather than in a sales inbox or a loose shared folder.

Close with one decision another team can use

The final step is a decision record:

  • Go: the route is stable, the supporting file matches the intended ingredient use, and the next team can move forward.
  • Hold: the route, file type, or downstream use is still unclear.
  • Revise: the claim may be workable, but the current wording or evidence packet is too broad or too vague.
  • Escalate: the decision now affects finished-label use, customer commitments, or a route change outside the original file.

That closeout keeps the statement from living forever as a vague "yes" inside the project folder.

Common Mistakes in Phosphatidylserine Vegan Review

Most failures are not scientific failures. They are scope failures.

  1. Treating any supplier email that says "vegan" as a finished-label approval.
  2. Using one generic PS statement while procurement is still switching between general, soy, and sunflower routes.
  3. Letting sales answer a customer vegan question before QA defines what the current file actually covers.
  4. Treating vegan review as a replacement for allergen, GMO, gluten-free, or packaging review.
  5. Reusing an old customer questionnaire answer across new formulas and new markets.
  6. Forgetting that U.S. and Canadian claim control still depends on substantiation, even when no premarket filing is required.

The practical fix is repeatable: keep one question at the center of the file. What exact phosphatidylserine route is supported, what evidence supports it, and what downstream use of the vegan statement is the current team actually approving?

Часто задаваемые вопросы

Does phosphatidylserine need a vegan statement for U.S. or Canadian buyers?

Not every project needs one, but many importers, distributors, and supplement brands request a vegan-support file when the downstream label, customer questionnaire, or channel positioning depends on it. The useful question is not only whether a file exists, but whether it matches the exact phosphatidylserine route and intended use.

Can a supplier's vegan statement be used directly on a finished supplement label?

Not automatically. A supplier file may support ingredient qualification, but finished-label review is broader and can depend on the rest of the formula, packaging decisions, and the exact wording a brand plans to use.

Is soy phosphatidylserine automatically excluded from vegan review because it is a soy allergen?

No. Vegan review and allergen review are separate tasks. A soy-derived ingredient may still need vegan substantiation, and a vegan-support file does not replace soy-allergen or source-declaration review.

What should buyers request before accepting a phosphatidylserine vegan claim?

Request the exact route-specific specification path, the current supporting statement or controlled questionnaire answer, and the commercial identity of the quoted phosphatidylserine route. Then define whether the file is for ingredient approval, customer support, or finished-label review.

How is a vegan statement different from GMO, gluten-free, or packaging review?

Those are separate claim or document workflows. A vegan statement focuses on whether the relevant route and downstream use are supported for vegan positioning. GMO, gluten-free, allergen, and packaging questions use different evidence and should be reviewed independently.
