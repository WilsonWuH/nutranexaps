## Introduction
Qualifying a phosphatidylserine (PS) supplier answers one question: is this source acceptable today? A supplier scorecard answers a different and more important question: is this source still performing six months and twenty lots later? For US and EU buyers of specialty phospholipid ingredients, most quality problems do not appear during qualification. They appear slowly, through drifting assay results, incomplete documentation packs, missed ship dates, and slower answers to technical questions. A lightweight quarterly scorecard catches that drift before it becomes a line-down event at your contract manufacturer or a customs hold at the border. This article defines the KPI categories, a starting weighting model, and how to convert scores into sourcing actions.

## Main Content

## Why a Supplier Scorecard Matters for Specialty Phospholipids

PS sits in a middle ground between commodity excipients and high-cost actives. The supply base is concentrated among a relatively small number of producers, the manufacturing route is an enzymatic conversion of lecithin, and the specification carries parameters that genuinely move between lots: phosphatidylserine assay, peroxide value, moisture, and microbiological status.

On the regulatory side, 21 CFR Part 111 requires US dietary supplement manufacturers to qualify suppliers and verify incoming material, while the FSVP rule places comparable verification duties on US importers. In the EU, buyers carry documentation and traceability duties under food law regardless of where the material was produced. A scorecard does not replace any of these obligations. It packages the evidence you already collect into a routine your team can actually sustain, and it turns scattered impressions about a supplier into a defensible record.

## The Four KPI Categories That Reveal PS Supplier Performance

**Quality.** The anchor metric is the COA-to-verification match rate: of the lots you tested in-house or via a third party, how many agreed with the supplier's certificate of analysis on identity, assay, and peroxide value? Track it alongside deviations per hundred lots and any out-of-specification events. A supplier can pass every incoming test and still be risky if you are retesting half of its lots.

**Documentation.** Score the percentage of shipments with a complete, first-time-right document pack: COA, allergen statement, SDS, country-of-origin information, and anything your market requires, such as GMO or irradiation statements. Incomplete packs surface at the worst possible moments — customs clearance, retail audits, or your own annual review.

**Delivery.** Measure on-time-in-full (OTIF) against confirmed dates, and separately track lead-time variance between purchase order and actual arrival. A supplier that ships on time but takes ten weeks longer than quoted each season is a planning risk even when nothing is late on paper.

**Responsiveness.** Record time to answer technical questions, time to issue a corrective action response after a complaint, and sample turnaround for new lot evaluations. In an ingredient recall or customer audit, responsiveness is the difference between a contained event and a broken contract.

## Weighting, Thresholds, and Review Cadence

A common starting model weights quality at 40 percent, delivery at 25 percent, documentation at 20 percent, and responsiveness at 15 percent. Adjust to your reality: if your contract manufacturer blends PS into finished goods for retail programs, documentation failures may cost you more than a late pallet.

Set thresholds that trigger defined responses rather than vague concern. For example, a COA match rate below 95 percent, two documentation gaps in a quarter, or OTIF below 90 percent each move a supplier to amber; any confirmed identity failure moves it straight to red. Whatever thresholds you pick, write them down before the first review and apply them the same way every quarter.

Score quarterly and review deeply once a year, ideally aligned with your annual supplier review and change-control process. Quarterly scoring should take one analyst an hour per supplier if your incoming inspection and shipping data are already organized.

## Turning Scorecard Results into Sourcing Decisions

![Procurement and QA teams reviewing supplier performance documents in a meeting room](/assets/images/resource-ps-supplier-scorecard-meeting.webp)

A scorecard only earns its keep when scores change behavior. Green suppliers earn more volume, annual agreements, and earlier involvement in new product briefs. Amber suppliers receive a corrective action request with a deadline, plus tightened incoming testing until two consecutive clean quarters. Red suppliers get a managed exit: you start qualifying a second source while fulfilling existing commitments.

For PS specifically, your second-source decision has a formulation dimension. If your primary supplier is soy-derived, sunflower-derived PS can serve as the allergen-friendly alternate, but you should verify label, spec, and regulatory documents for the alternate before you need it — not during a disruption. Record every scorecard-driven decision in the supplier file so the rationale survives staff turnover and customer audits.

## Common Pitfalls When Scoring PS Suppliers

The first pitfall is scoring on price and delivery alone; cheap material with unstable documentation is expensive in disguise. The second is collecting scores that nobody acts on — if red has no defined consequence, the scorecard becomes theater. The third is silence: share results with the supplier each quarter. Good PS producers want the feedback, and the ones that do not are telling you something. Finally, do not let the scorecard drift into a spreadsheet nobody owns. Assign a named owner in procurement or QA and put the quarterly review on the calendar like any other quality activity.

## FAQ
### Q: How soon after onboarding should we start scoring a PS supplier?
A: Start formally after the first three commercial lots, once you have verified incoming data. Earlier than that, sample-stage results are too thin to score fairly; later, you lose a quarter of early-warning value.

### Q: Should we share the scorecard with our supplier?
A: Yes, in summary form. Sharing the category scores and the trend, without internal cost or strategy notes, usually improves responsiveness and gives the supplier a fair chance to correct issues before sourcing decisions change.

### Q: Can a scorecard replace a supplier audit?
A: No. A scorecard is continuous monitoring between audits; an audit verifies the management system on site. Use scorecard trends to decide audit frequency and to focus the audit agenda on weak areas.

### Q: Which single KPI matters most for phosphatidylserine?
A: COA-to-verification match rate. PS specifications are sensitive to assay and oxidative markers, so systematic agreement between supplier certificates and your own verification is the cleanest signal of a controlled process.

## Conclusion
A phosphatidylserine supplier scorecard is a small investment with an outsized payoff: one analyst-hour per quarter per supplier, in exchange for early warning on quality drift, documentation gaps, and delivery risk. Define four KPI categories, set written thresholds, score quarterly, review annually, and — most importantly — let the scores change how you allocate volume. Your FSVP or Part 111 obligations will feel far less abstract when the evidence is already organized.
