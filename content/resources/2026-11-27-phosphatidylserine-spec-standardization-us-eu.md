## Introduction

Most brands do not decide to run multiple phosphatidylserine specifications — they accumulate them. A cognitive-health capsule launched years ago carries the supplier spec its contract manufacturer copied into the quality agreement. A newer gummy SKU inherits a different sheet drafted by a different co-packer. A soy-free line sources sunflower-derived PS under yet another document, and a distributor slot buys against the supplier's standard grade sheet. By the time procurement actually compares the files, the same ingredient is being bought, tested and released under three or four divergent documents.

That divergence is not just paperwork. Divergent specs mean divergent incoming tests, wider effective tolerances than any single sheet describes, harder supplier comparisons, and audit findings when the dossier does not match what the plant actually releases against. Standardization fixes the problem at the document level, and it starts with a clear view of the ingredient itself — its grades, sources and typical parameters, which you can review in the [phosphatidylserine ingredient specifications](/products/phosphatidylserine/) overview.

## Main Content

### Why PS Specifications Proliferate Across a Portfolio

Specification drift has predictable causes. New-product teams often draft a spec from the first supplier's quotation instead of the company's established ingredient standard. Contract manufacturers insert their own incoming-inspection parameters into quality agreements. Formulation changes — a move from 50% PS powder to a higher-concentration grade, or from soy to sunflower source — get patched onto the old sheet rather than replacing it. And legacy specs survive simply because nobody owns the portfolio view: each SKU's document is managed by a different QA or purchasing contact.

The cost shows up in three places. First, incoming testing: each distinct spec can trigger distinct test panels, so the same powder gets assayed differently depending on which SKU it was purchased for. Second, supplier management: a supplier asked to meet three slightly different sets of limits will quote against the loosest interpretation, and buyers lose negotiating leverage because comparisons rest on non-identical documents. Third, compliance review: when a US customer audit or an EU label check traces a lot back through the dossier, mismatched spec versions raise questions the ingredient itself never deserved.

### What Standardization Actually Means for an Ingredient Like PS

Standardization does not mean one document for every situation. PS differs by source (soy-derived versus sunflower-derived), by concentration, and by presentation (plain powder versus carrier-blended or microencapsulated powder) — and those differences are legitimate. Harmonization means establishing a master specification per ingredient family, so that every parameter either comes from the master or is a documented, justified deviation.

In practice a master PS specification defines the shared core: identity tests that confirm the phospholipid, the assay method and its acceptance window, peroxide-value and moisture limits, particle-size and flowability parameters where the dosage form needs them, and the documentation package each lot must carry. SKU-specific rows — allergen labeling needs, carrier identity, or a tighter microbiological window for a children's product — then sit on top as controlled annexes. The discipline buyers gain from this structure mirrors the work of building an acceptance specification panel for a single grade; the difference is scope, as our guide to [building an acceptance specification panel](/resources/phosphatidylserine-acceptance-specification-panel-us-eu/) explains.

Standardization also forces the source question into the open. Where SKUs span soy and sunflower PS, the master document should state explicitly whether the two sources share limits or carry separate annexes — blending the two into one ambiguous sheet is a common audit finding.

### Building the Master Specification

Start with a spec inventory. Pull every phosphatidylserine specification in circulation — quality agreements with contract manufacturers, purchase-order attachments, distributor agreements, and internal incoming-inspection standards. Tabulate them row by row: parameter, method, unit, limit. The table itself usually settles the argument, because it exposes limits that differ only by drafting history rather than by product need.

For each row, decide one of three outcomes: adopt the tighter limit as the master, adopt the looser limit where no product justification for the tighter one exists, or classify the row as SKU-specific and move it to an annex. Rows where methods — not just limits — differ deserve extra attention, because a limit is meaningless without a fixed method; assay and oxidative-stability parameters are the usual suspects, and our comparison of [PS concentration grades](/resources/phosphatidylserine-concentration-grades-compared-us-eu/) shows how grade and method choices interact. The output is one controlled master document with version control, a change log, and named annexes per SKU or market.

### Aligning Suppliers with the Harmonized Spec

A master specification only works if suppliers and contract manufacturers sign up to it. Treat the rollout as a supplier-notification event under your change-control procedure, not a silent document swap. For each supplier, map their current grade against the master: most parameters should already conform; the gaps are typically method references, documentation requirements, or limits they never formally agreed to.

Where a supplier's standard grade sits outside the master limit, buyers have three honest options: negotiate the change, keep a documented annex for that supplier, or move the volume. Avoid the informal fourth option — letting the old sheet live on "temporarily" — because it recreates the drift you just removed. Contract manufacturers need the harmonized spec inserted into the quality agreement with the incoming-test panel stated explicitly, so their release criteria match yours rather than their template's. Under US dietary supplement CGMP, the brand owner remains responsible for establishing ingredient specifications and verifying them; 21 CFR Part 111 puts that duty on the finished-goods company regardless of who runs the tests, which is exactly why the master document should be yours, not the co-packer's.

### Rolling Out the Change Without Disrupting Production

Sequence matters. Standardizing mid-stream can invalidate open purchase orders, force re-testing of in-transit lots, or clash with a contract manufacturer's validated incoming procedure. A workable rollout runs in three waves: new SKUs and new suppliers adopt the master specification immediately; open orders finish against the documents they were placed under, with the change logged; and renewals — annual quality-agreement reviews, contract re-signings, supplier requalifications — become the natural moment to swap each legacy sheet for the master.

Set an internal effective date and track the conversion. The goal at the end of the wave is simple: every PS purchase order in the system references either the master specification or a named, justified annex. Warehouses and incoming-inspection teams need the new test panel before the first lots arrive against it, and the certificate-of-analysis review process should be updated so reviewers check against the master limits, not a folder of historical sheets.

### Common Pitfalls in Spec Harmonization

The recurring failures are process failures. Copying a supplier's grade sheet into the master without independent review locks in the supplier's tolerances and hides method differences. Standardizing on the loosest limits across the portfolio to avoid supplier pushback quietly widens every SKU's quality gate. Annexes without owners multiply again within a year, because nobody retires them when the SKU dies. And harmonizing documents without harmonizing the incoming-test plan leaves QA testing against an old panel while release records claim the new spec.

Two disciplines prevent most of this: a single owner for the master specification with change control, and a quarterly portfolio sweep that lists every active PS spec, its owner, and its expiry — the same review rhythm used for supplier scorecards. In the EU, keep Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011 labeling requirements in view when consolidating annexes, since allergen and source declarations tie directly to which annex a SKU carries.

## FAQ

### Why do different SKUs end up with different phosphatidylserine specifications if the powder is the same?

Because each spec was drafted at a different time by a different party — a contract manufacturer's template, a first-supplier quotation, or a legacy sheet patched through formulation changes. The ingredient is the same; the drafting history is not. Harmonization replaces drafting history with one controlled master document.

### Does standardization force us to buy one grade of PS for everything?

No. A master specification controls how limits and methods are expressed and which deviations are allowed; it does not collapse grades. Source (soy or sunflower), concentration and carrier presentations remain separate choices, handled as controlled annexes rather than separate ad-hoc documents.

### How do we handle a supplier whose standard grade does not meet the harmonized limit?

Three legitimate routes: negotiate the supplier up to the master limit, keep a documented supplier-specific annex with an owner and expiry, or move the volume. The one option to avoid is letting the legacy sheet persist informally, which recreates the original drift.

### Can we standardize specs without re-testing already-purchased inventory?

Yes, if the rollout is sequenced properly. Open orders are typically completed against the documents they were placed under, and the master specification applies from an effective date to new orders — subject to your quality agreement terms and any commitments made to contract manufacturers.

## Conclusion

Spec proliferation is a quiet tax on every phosphatidylserine programme: extra testing, weaker supplier comparisons, and audit exposure that the ingredient itself never earned. A master specification with controlled annexes, a sequenced supplier rollout, and a quarterly portfolio sweep turn that tax into a single controlled document set.
