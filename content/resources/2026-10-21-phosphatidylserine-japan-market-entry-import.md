## Introduction

Japan rarely anchors a phosphatidylserine market-entry plan at global headquarters, yet it is one of the most mature cognitive-health markets in the world: the oldest demographic profile of any major economy, a health-food culture that mainstreamed memory and focus ingredients decades ago, and retail channels where brain-health formats are an established category. Japan also runs a regulatory system with its own grammar: food-supplement oversight anchored in the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare (MHLW), a functionality-notification system that looks deceptively light until you examine the evidence behind it, and a Food Sanitation Act import notification that every shipment must pass. A PS grade that clears US and EU review without friction can still stall at a Japanese quarantine station if the notification paperwork and the ingredient file do not line up.

This guide is written for sourcing managers and brand owners evaluating Japan for the first time. It walks through the regulator landscape and the Foods with Functional Claims (FFC) notification system, the import notification route through the Quarantine Station, and the documentation to demand from your ingredient supplier before the first shipment. The recurring theme will be familiar: verify current requirements in official Japanese sources rather than assuming that Western or regional clearances transfer, because in Japan they often do not.

## Main Content

### Why Japan is a distinct PS opportunity in East Asia

Japan's consumer health market rests on demographics few countries can match: roughly three in ten citizens are over 65, and cognitive wellness sits at the intersection of healthy-aging ambition and one of the world's highest per-capita health-food spending levels. The supplement category is mature and quality-literate: buyers expect Japanese-language information by default and reward brands with consistent documentation histories. Domestic production of specialty phospholipids is effectively nonexistent, so PS demand is import-dependent: brands source the ingredient through importers or contract manufacturers who carry the regulatory notifications and import paperwork. The commercial upside is real, but so is the operational floor: quality expectations at entry are among the strictest in Asia, and landed-cost modeling must account for duties, port handling, and the lead time that notification and documentation review add to any launch. Buyers who treat Japan as "another APAC market" usually discover the difference at the document stage; the regional context for the neighboring market is covered in our [Japan and Korea market entry guide](/resources/phosphatidylserine-japan-korea-market-entry-import/).

### Japan's supplement framework: MHLW oversight and the FFC notification system

Japan regulates foods and supplements through the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare, with the Pharmaceuticals and Medical Devices Agency (PMDA) relevant wherever a product's presentation drifts toward drug-like claims. Within the food category, the labeling and functionality framework that matters most for PS is the Foods with Functional Claims (FFC) system, administered under the food-labeling rules overseen with the Consumer Affairs Agency. FFC sits between two older tiers: foods with nutrient function claims and Foods for Specified Health Uses (FOSHU), the latter requiring pre-market approval. FFC products do not need pre-market approval, but every product must be notified with a dossier of scientific evidence — safety information, functionality substantiation, and quality-management details — and notified products face review windows and post-market scrutiny.

For an ingredient buyer, the strategic takeaway is upstream: the finished-product notification inherits the ingredient file. Phosphatidylserine's identity, source, specifications, and the safety and functionality evidence at the target dose all feed the notification, and gaps at ingredient level surface as deficiencies at notification level — typically after the brand has committed to artwork and launch dates. Because FFC claims must stay within what the submitted evidence supports, the ingredient supplier's study literature and specification discipline directly shape what the finished product is allowed to say on shelf.

### Import notification and customs clearance through the Quarantine Station

Every shipment of PS entering Japan as a food ingredient — or as a finished supplement — passes through the import notification process under the Food Sanitation Act. The importer of record must submit a Notification of Food Import to the Quarantine Station with jurisdiction over the port or airport of entry, generally after arrival and before customs clearance proceeds through the NACCS electronic system. Quarantine Station officials examine the notification documents against the declared goods and can escalate to monitoring or laboratory inspection; document quality and the importer's compliance history strongly influence how often shipments are sampled. For PS specifically, the ingredient is treated as a food under the Food Sanitation Act, while Japan's positive-list system for pesticide residues forms the background against which certificates are read.

For a foreign ingredient supplier, the practical implications mirror other tightly controlled markets: your counterpart's import track record is part of your risk chain, the notification details must match the commercial invoice, specification, and certificates exactly, and who files the notification and bears import risk should be written into the supply or distribution agreement. Foreign suppliers do not file import notifications themselves — choosing a Japanese importer that handles food-category notifications routinely is the single highest-leverage decision in the market entry plan. Timelines reward preparation: notification filings, inspection scheduling, and customs entry each run on their own windows, so aligning them during partner qualification keeps the launch calendar from slipping at the border.

### Specifications, JHFA context and Japanese labeling expectations

The ingredient document pack for Japan builds on the same backbone used for US and EU shipments, with two material additions: evidence alignment for the FFC notification and tolerance for closer scrutiny. The core set: a current specification stating identity, assay, and physical parameters; a per-batch COA consistent with that specification; allergen declarations (soy-derived PS is, explicitly, soy, and soy is a mandatory allergen display item in Japan); GMO status statements; manufacturing site quality certification; and the safety and functionality literature supporting the ingredient's use at the intended dose. Outside the statutory system, the Japan Health Food & Nutrition Food Association (JHFA) maintains voluntary standards and a certification mark for health foods; it is not a legal requirement, but JHFA norms describe the quality bar sophisticated Japanese channel partners often expect. Labeling is the consistent stall point: the Food Labeling Act framework requires Japanese-language mandatory information, nutritional and allergen display in the prescribed formats, and functionality statements that stay within what the FFC notification supports — artwork drift beyond the notified evidence is an enforcement exposure, not a marketing nuance. Document discipline is decisive because reviewers cross-check consistency across the notification, certificates, and label; the same practice that keeps multi-market launches aligned is described in our [COA and specification review checklist](/resources/phosphatidylserine-coa-specification-certificate-review/).

## FAQ

### Q: Does phosphatidylserine require product registration in Japan?

**A:** The ingredient itself is not registered separately, but a finished product making functional claims must be notified under the Foods with Functional Claims system with its evidence dossier before marketing, and every food import requires notification under the Food Sanitation Act. Buyers should confirm PS's current treatment at the intended dose and claim set with the published FFC rules and, where needed, a Japanese regulatory consultant.

### Q: Can a foreign company file the import notification and clear goods in Japan directly?

**A:** No. The import notification under the Food Sanitation Act is filed by the importer of record — a Japanese entity with customs registration operating through the NACCS system. Foreign suppliers work with Japanese importers, trading houses, or distributors, and should contractually clarify who files notifications and bears import risk.

### Q: Which supplier documents does a Japanese importer need for PS?

**A:** The core pack is the specification, per-batch COA, allergen and GMO declarations, manufacturing site quality certification, and the safety and functionality literature that supports the finished product's FFC notification. Consistency across all documents — names, grades, sites, and notified content — matters as much as completeness, because mismatches routinely trigger inspection escalation.

### Q: How long does Japan market entry take for a PS supplement?

**A:** Timelines vary with claim scope and dossier quality: FFC notification with its evidence review window, artwork approval, and per-shipment import notification each add weeks, and functionality substantiation is often the longest pole. Planning the ingredient document pack in parallel with importer selection — rather than after it — is the most reliable way to compress the calendar.
