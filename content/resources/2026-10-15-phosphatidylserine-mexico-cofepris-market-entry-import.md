## Introduction

Mexico has become one of the most attractive growth markets for dietary supplements in Latin America, and cognitive-health ingredients such as phosphatidylserine (PS) are appearing in local formulations for professionals, students, and aging consumers. For a US or EU brand owner, Mexico is a near-market: freight is short, USMCA trade terms are favorable, and distribution culture is familiar. Yet operationally it is not a copy-paste of the US model. The competent authority, COFEPRIS (Comisión Federal para la Protección contra Riesgos Sanitarios), applies its own sanitary framework to imported supplements and ingredients, and many first-time importers find that paperwork — not freight — determines their timeline.

This guide is written for the B2B buyer: the importer of record, the brand launching a PS product in Mexico, and the contract manufacturer sourcing PS powder for a Mexican client. It covers how COFEPRIS treats phosphatidylserine, the registrations needed before the first shipment, the documents accompanying each lot, and how to build a supplier documentation pack that makes sanitary clearance routine.

## How COFEPRIS Regulates Phosphatidylserine

COFEPRIS regulates products, facilities, and imports through sanitary control rather than a pre-market approval system identical to the US NDI notification or the EU novel food process. The analysis starts with the product category: a bulk PS powder sold to a Mexican manufacturer is handled differently from a retail-packed PS softgel or gummy. Finished supplements fall under the suplementos alimenticios framework, which defines permitted ingredients, dose ceilings, and mandatory label statements.

PS derived from soy or sunflower lecithin is well established as a food and supplement ingredient, and its long history of use supports a straightforward category discussion. Still, confirm the ingredient's status for your exact finished format and dose with a regulatory advisor in Mexico before locking a specification, because category rules — not ingredient science — drive the approval path.

COFEPRIS oversight does not stop at the border. Imported lots remain traceable to the importer of record, who must show on request where each lot went and which documents support it. The supplier's batch documentation is therefore a live compliance asset in Mexico, not a warehouse formality.

## Importer-of-Record Setup: Licenses and Sectorial Register

The first structural decision is who acts as importer of record. Foreign brands typically choose between a Mexican distributor that already holds sanitary licenses, or a third-party import agent that clears goods on the brand's behalf. Either way, the entity must be constituted for sanitary purposes before the PS ships, because sanitary import documents are issued against that entity, not the supplier abroad.

Three building blocks are required. First, a current operating notice or sanitary license covering storage and distribution of food supplements. Second, enrollment in the sectorial importers' register (padrón sectorial), which links the company's federal taxpayer registration to the sanitary authority's systems. Third, a licensed customs broker (agente aduanal); choosing one with food-supplement experience matters as much as choosing the right freight forwarder.

Start this setup during supplier selection, not after the purchase order. Registration steps can take weeks, and no supplier will release a commercial shipment against an importer whose credentials are still in process.

## The Sanitary Import Permit and Core Documentation

![A regulatory affairs specialist at a modern trade compliance desk reviewing printed sanitary import permit documents next to a laptop, with a sealed foil pouch of white nutritional powder resting on a glass tray, soft daylight, photorealistic, 16:9](/assets/images/resource-ps-mexico-cofepris-a.webp)

Depending on classification, PS shipments may require a sanitary import permit (permiso sanitario de importación) issued by COFEPRIS before arrival, in addition to the customs entry. The application draws on a defined document set, and your supplier's paperwork quality determines whether approval takes days or weeks. Assemble at minimum:

- Commercial invoice and packing list consistent with the permit application.
- A certificate of analysis per lot covering assay, peroxide value, moisture, heavy metals, and microbiology.
- A free sale certificate or GMP attestation from the country of manufacture, usually legalized or apostilled for Mexico.
- Manufacturing-site information, food-safety certifications, and a letter confirming food/supplement use.
- Spanish labeling proofs for retail-packed goods.

The most common failure mode is inconsistency: the invoice names one grade, the COA another, and the permit application a third. Reviewers read these documents as a single record, so require a "Mexico document pack" per lot with names, lot numbers, and quantities verified against each other before dispatch — the same document-consistency discipline buyers apply to EU and US shipments.

## Spanish Labeling and NOM-051 Alignment

Retail packaging must comply with the general labeling standard NOM-051-SSA1-2010 and the supplement-specific rules. That means Spanish-language mandatory information — product identity, net content, ingredients, lot code, storage conditions, and legally required statements — in the specified typography and placement. Health claims are tightly restricted: wording permitted for a US structure-function label will not survive review in Mexico, so plan claims against the suplementos alimenticios framework, not a direct translation.

Buyers have two routes. Importing in bulk and packing in Mexico shifts labeling to the local contract packer but requires a validated line handoff. Importing retail-ready goods requires print-ready Spanish artwork and an in-country legal representative. Both work; problems arise when the decision is made late. Artwork, translation, and claim review cycles routinely add six to eight weeks, so fix the labeling route together with format and formulation.

## Common Pitfalls That Delay PS Shipments at Mexican Ports

![A warehouse quality technician in a white coat inspecting blue plastic drums of nutritional powder stacked on pallets near a loading dock, holding a clipboard and checking a drum label, warm industrial lighting, photorealistic, 16:9](/assets/images/resource-ps-mexico-cofepris-b.webp)

Recurring, avoidable delays reported by Mexico importers apply equally to PS:

- Permits applied for after the vessel sails, leaving goods to accrue demurrage at port.
- Apostilled or legalized documents arriving late because suppliers ignore that lead time.
- Label proofs changed after printing, forcing relabeling at destination.
- Lot numbers that do not reconcile across COA, invoice, permit, and drum labels, triggering holds.
- Temperature exposure: PS is oxidation-sensitive, and summer port heat combined with customs dwell argues for nitrogen-flushed packaging and a pre-agreed inspection timeline.

Each is a planning failure rather than a regulatory impossibility. Build a Mexico-specific pre-shipment gate — permit status, document pack verification, artwork sign-off, packaging spec confirmation — and refuse to ship until every item is green.

## Building a Supplier Documentation Pack for Mexico

Turn this into a repeatable routine by defining Mexico as a destination pack within the quality agreement: which documents the supplier issues per lot, in what format, with apostille where required, and how far ahead each must arrive. Screen prospective PS suppliers with three questions: Have you shipped to Mexico or LATAM? Can you support apostilled free sale documentation from your country of manufacture? Can your COA formats reconcile exactly with invoice line names? Suppliers with real experience answer with specifics — port pairs, permit workflows, broker contacts — while inexperienced ones answer with reassurance. That difference is a free qualification signal at RFQ stage.

## FAQ

### What is a sanitary import permit for phosphatidylserine in Mexico?

A sanitary import permit (permiso sanitario de importación) is a COFEPRIS authorization required for certain health-sector goods before they enter Mexico. For PS raw material or finished supplements, it links the product, quantity, and importer to the sanitary system, and must be granted before shipment so clearance is not delayed at port.

### Does my Mexican distributor need special registration to import PS?

Yes. The importing entity must hold the appropriate sanitary operating notice or license for storing and distributing food supplements, be enrolled in the sectorial importers' register (padrón sectorial), and work with a licensed customs broker. Confirm all three before confirming production with your PS supplier.

### Do I need to legalize my supplier's free sale certificate for Mexico?

Usually yes, or an apostille must be obtained, depending on the issuing country and current Mexican requirements. Plan for this lead time during supplier qualification, because legalization timelines are outside both parties' control and frequently surprise first-time importers.

### Can I import PS supplements with English-only labels for sale in Mexico?

No. Retail-packaged supplements must carry Spanish-language mandatory information under NOM-051-SSA1-2010 and the supplement-specific rules, and claims must be permissible in Mexico; a direct translation of US structure-function wording is not acceptable.

### Is soy-derived PS acceptable in Mexican supplements, or should I use sunflower PS?

Both soy-derived and sunflower-derived PS exist in the market; the choice depends on product positioning, allergen strategy, and the finished format and dose. Confirm ingredient status for your exact format with a Mexican regulatory advisor, then lock the specification with your supplier.

## Conclusion

Mexico rewards PS buyers who treat it as a distinct regulatory market rather than an extension of the US. The pillars: an importer of record with the right sanitary registrations and a supplement-experienced customs broker; sanitary import permits applied for before shipment; a per-lot document pack that reconciles line by line; Spanish labeling and claims aligned to NOM-051; and a supplier who has proven LATAM documentation capability. Start the permit and registration workstreams in parallel with supplier selection, freeze Spanish artwork early, and favor suppliers who offer apostille-ready certificates and consistent COA naming — in the Mexican sanitary system, the cheapest shipment is the one that clears the first time.
