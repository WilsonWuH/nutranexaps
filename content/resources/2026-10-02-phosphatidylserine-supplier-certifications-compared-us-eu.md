## Introduction

A phosphatidylserine supplier's qualification file usually opens with a page of certificate scans: ISO 22000, FSSC 22000, HACCP, ISO 9001, halal, kosher. Buyers file them, tick a box, and move on — and the qualification decision quietly rests on documents nobody has read. The opposite error is just as common: treating certificates as marketing wallpaper and skipping to price, then discovering at audit or during an FDA inspection that the supplier's system has gaps a certificate would have predicted.

![Procurement specialist reviewing a folder of supplier certification documents at a desk in a modern office](/assets/images/resource-ps-phosphatidylserine-supplier-certifications-compared-us-eu-docs.webp)

Certificates are evidence, and each type is evidence of something specific. Read them as four separate layers — what the scheme audits, what regulatory regime it touches, which site it covers and when it expires — and they become one of the fastest pre-audit filters available to a PS buyer. This article unpacks each layer and ends with a verification checklist.

## Main Content

## The Four Layers: Safety System, Management System, Regulatory Regime, Scheme

Separate four things buyers habitually merge. First, the food safety system: hazard analysis, CCPs, prerequisites — the operational machinery that keeps contaminants out of product. Second, the management system: document control, internal audits, management review, CAPA discipline — the machinery that keeps the first machinery running. Third, the regulatory regime: the product law the material must satisfy in your market, such as US dietary supplement cGMP or EU food law — a certificate does not substitute for this, it only supports it. Fourth, the scheme itself: who wrote the standard, who accredits the audit body, and how much auditor discretion it allows. A certificate tells you a third party checked a defined scope against a defined standard on a defined date. Most qualification mistakes come from assuming it covers one of the other three layers.

## FSSC 22000 and ISO 22000: Same Family, Different Depth

ISO 22000 defines the requirements for a food safety management system (FSMS) — hazard analysis per Codex principles, prerequisite programmes, traceability, control of nonconforming product. Any accredited certification body can audit against it. FSSC 22000 builds on ISO 22000 but adds sector-specific prerequisite requirements for food manufacturing, additional scheme requirements — food fraud mitigation, food defense, allergen and environmental monitoring expectations — and, critically, is benchmarked and recognized by GFSI, the Global Food Safety Initiative.

For a PS buyer the practical read is: FSSC 22000 is the deeper audit with external integrity checks, and it is the certificate most large US and EU food and supplement manufacturers prefer for ingredient suppliers. ISO 22000 alone is a legitimate FSMS audit, but it is not GFSI-recognized, scheme discipline and auditor depth vary more, and it says nothing about the sector-specific prerequisites a lipid manufacturer needs. When a supplier holds ISO 22000 but not a GFSI scheme, ask why — sometimes the answer is cost or market focus, sometimes it is that they would not pass the additional requirements.

## HACCP: The Foundation, Not the Building

HACCP is not a certification scheme in the same sense — it is the hazard analysis and control methodology underneath every modern food safety standard, and "HACCP-certified" usually means a short third-party course or a basic system audit rather than the multi-day full-scope audit behind FSSC 22000. Treat a HACCP certificate as a floor signal: the supplier has identified hazards and CCPs. Do not treat it as equivalent to a GFSI certificate or let it stand in for reviewing the hazard analysis itself. For a phospholipid process the meaningful HACCP content is in the CCPs tied to your risk profile — solvent removal, oxidative control, foreign body and metal detection, and allergen segregation. Ask for the HACCP plan summary, not just the certificate.

## cGMP Under 21 CFR Part 111: The US Supplement Question

Here is the misreading that costs buyers the most: assuming a GFSI food safety certificate means the material is produced under FDA dietary supplement cGMP. It does not. FSSC 22000 audits a food safety system against ISO-based requirements; 21 CFR Part 111 imposes US regulatory requirements specific to dietary supplement ingredients — established specifications, supplier qualification, batch production records, laboratory controls, and the identity/purity/strength/composition framework FDA inspectors check. A supplier can hold FSSC 22000 and never have designed their documentation for Part 111 — no established specifications per batch, no master batch records, no qualified-supplier program in the FDA sense.

If your PS enters a US supplement, your manufacturer's own Part 111 obligations require them to qualify you as a supplier, which flows down: ask the PS supplier directly whether their quality system supports Part 111 customer requirements — batch records, specification establishment, and supporting an FDA inspection trail. A supplier who answers with cGMP audit reports is worth more to a US supplement program than one more certificate on the wall. EU buyers have the mirror-image question: does the supplier's documentation support EU food law and member-state requirements, including the food business registration expectations of Regulation (EC) 178/2002.

## Verifying Certificates: Scope, Validity and the Checks That Catch Fakes

Certificate fraud is rare; scope carelessness is common. Verify four things on every certificate. Expiry and audit cycle: certificates expire on a date and recertification audits happen on a three-year cycle — an expired scan in a qualification file is a red flag about the supplier's document discipline. Scope statement: the certificate names the site address and the activity audited (e.g. "manufacture of phospholipid ingredients"); a certificate for "trading of food ingredients" does not cover a manufacturing site. Accreditation mark: the certification body must be accredited by a recognized IAF-signatory accreditation member — check the CB's status on the accreditation body's public register. Scheme register: GFSI certificate holders are listed in public databases. Five minutes on the scheme register catches the expired certificate and the unaccredited audit body before they reach your approved supplier list.

## Combining the Evidence into a Qualification Decision

Use certificates as a filter, not a verdict. A sensible weighting for a PS supplier: GFSI certification (FSSC 22000 or equivalent) as the baseline for a manufacturing site; evidence the quality system supports your market's regime (Part 111 support for US supplements, EU food law documentation for Europe) as the deciding layer certificates alone cannot prove; then your own verification — questionnaire, COA history, and for meaningful volumes an on-site or third-party audit. score the layers separately and patterns emerge: the supplier with ISO 9001 and halal but no GFSI scheme may be a trading house; the supplier with FSSC 22000 but no Part 111 documentation may be an excellent food-ingredient producer needing a supplement-focused qualification project before your program can use them. Both are usable findings — but only if you read the certificates as what they are.

## FAQ

### Q: What certifications should a phosphatidylserine supplier have?

A: At minimum a GFSI-recognized food safety certificate such as FSSC 22000 for the manufacturing site, plus market-specific support: documentation that enables US dietary supplement cGMP qualification for US supplement programs, and EU food law documentation for European customers. Halal, kosher and allergen-related certifications matter when your positioning requires them.

### Q: Is FSSC 22000 better than ISO 22000?

A: FSSC 22000 includes ISO 22000 plus sector-specific prerequisite requirements, food fraud and food defense requirements, and GFSI recognition — so it is the deeper, externally benchmarked audit. ISO 22000 alone is a legitimate FSMS certificate but is not GFSI-recognized.

### Q: Does a food safety certificate prove cGMP compliance?

A: No. FSSC 22000 or ISO 22000 audits a food safety management system; US dietary supplement cGMP under 21 CFR Part 111 is a separate regulatory regime with its own specification, batch record and supplier qualification requirements. Ask specifically whether the supplier's system supports Part 111 customer requirements.

### Q: How do I verify a supplier's certification is valid?

A: Check the expiry date, the certificate scope (site address and audited activity), the certification body's accreditation via a recognized (IAF-signatory) accreditation register, and the scheme's public holder database for GFSI certificates.

### Q: What is the difference between HACCP and a GFSI certificate?

A: HACCP is the hazard analysis methodology underlying all modern food safety standards; a "HACCP certificate" typically reflects a basic system audit or training. A GFSI certificate like FSSC 22000 involves a full multi-day site audit against a comprehensive standard with scheme-level integrity checks.

## Conclusion

Certifications are the fastest pre-audit filter a phosphatidylserine buyer has — if each certificate is read as evidence of a specific layer. FSSC 22000 tells you the site passed a GFSI-benchmarked food safety audit; ISO 22000 alone tells you less; HACCP tells you a hazard analysis exists; none of them proves your US cGMP or EU documentation obligations are covered — you establish that by asking directly. Verify scope and validity, weight the layers separately, and confirm with your own audit where volumes justify it.
