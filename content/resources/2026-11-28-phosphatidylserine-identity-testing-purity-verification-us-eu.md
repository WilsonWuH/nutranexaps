## Introduction

A phosphatidylserine certificate of analysis can be internally consistent, lot-linked and method-cited — and the powder can still not be what the invoice says it is. The realistic misrepresentation patterns for a purified phospholipid ingredient are substitution (replacing part of the PS with phosphatidylcholine or a lecithin-derived fraction) and dilution (stretching the powder with carriers while keeping the declared grade). Both exploit the same weakness: buyers who treat "identity" as a checkbox on the COA rather than a laboratory question with method choices of its own.

This article is the methods guide: which techniques actually confirm that a powder is phosphatidylserine — a class-resolved HPLC assay, HPTLC fingerprinting, ³¹P NMR — what each can and cannot prove, and how to cross-check the COA against the specification before releasing a lot. Under US dietary supplement CGMP, the brand owner must establish and verify the identity of each incoming ingredient, so the method decision sits with the buyer, not the supplier. Start from the [phosphatidylserine ingredient specifications](/products/phosphatidylserine/) for the grade you are validating, because every identity claim is only meaningful against a defined spec.

## Main Content

### What Substitution and Dilution Actually Look Like in a PS Powder

Phosphatidylcholine (PC) is the natural suspect, because it is the cheapest phospholipid class in the supply chain — it dominates deoiled lecithin — while PS is made by enzymatic transphosphatidylation from a phospholipid feedstock and is far more valuable. Substitution means blending PC-rich material into a PS powder so the blend still reports a plausible phospholipid content; dilution means stretching the powder with carriers or feed lecithin while invoicing it at the declared concentration. Both keep the total phospholipid number healthy, which is exactly why a "total phospholipids" figure on its own proves nothing about PS. Any credible verification must resolve phosphatidylserine as a molecular species, not as a sum: a batch can show 70% "phospholipids" while containing well under half that as actual PS. Every method below answers one question — does this technique separate PS from its neighboring phospholipid classes, or does it only see phosphorus?

### The HPLC Assay: Quantifying PS as Such, Not Total Phospholipids

The workhorse for PS purity is HPLC with class separation — evaporative light-scattering (ELSD) or charged-aerosol (CAD) detection — which resolves the phospholipid spectrum into its classes and quantifies phosphatidylserine specifically. A properly executed run shows PS as the dominant peak alongside residual PC, phosphatidylethanolamine, phosphatidylinositol and phosphatidic acid. This is the method that catches substitution directly: PC cut into PS shows up as an inflated PC peak, and dilution with lecithin shows up as a spectrum that looks like feedstock rather than converted PS.

The failure mode to avoid is method ambiguity. UV-only or total-phosphorus approaches read every phospholipid class together and inflate the headline number — a 50% grade assayed on a total basis can pass its limit while containing far less actual PS. When commissioning a test, require the chromatogram, not just the result, and confirm the method resolves PS from PC. The validation parameters that make such a method defensible are covered in our guide to [HPLC assay method validation for PS](/resources/phosphatidylserine-hplc-assay-method-validation-us-eu/), and the phospholipid spectrum also carries the fatty-acid source fingerprint discussed in our [identity testing and fatty acid profile guide](/resources/phosphatidylserine-identity-testing-fatty-acid-profile-us-eu/).

### Identity Methods: HPTLC and ³¹P NMR as Orthogonal Confirmation

HPTLC (high-performance thin-layer chromatography) is the classic identity method for phospholipid ingredients: the sample and a reference standard are developed on the same plate, derivatized, and the spot patterns are compared for position and intensity. It answers the identity question — is this material's phospholipid pattern consistent with PS — quickly and visually, which makes it a good incoming-inspection screen and a first tool when a lot looks wrong. Its limits are quantitative: it confirms pattern consistency far better than it measures exact PS content, so a passing HPTLC pattern does not by itself certify the assay figure.

³¹P NMR (phosphorus-31 nuclear magnetic resonance) works from the other direction. Because each phospholipid class has a characteristically shifted phosphorus resonance, an NMR spectrum quantifies every class simultaneously without chromatographic separation — PS, PC, PE, PI and PA each appear as their own signal. It is the strongest single answer to substitution: a PC-blended or lecithin-diluted powder reveals itself in the class ratios. The trade-offs are practical — fewer labs run it routinely and cost per sample is higher — so most programmes use it selectively: new-supplier qualification, dispute testing, or lots where HPLC and HPTLC disagree.

A layered programme is the practical answer: HPLC class-resolved assay for purity on every tested lot, HPTLC as the identity screen, and ³¹P NMR as the orthogonal check when the stakes or inconsistencies justify it. Confirm method availability and reporting format with your testing laboratory before the sample ships.

### Cross-Checking the COA Against the Specification

Laboratory results only become verification when they are judged against a defined specification. The cross-check is a coherence exercise:

- **Assay basis.** Does the COA state PS as such, or total phospholipids? A 70% figure on a total-phospholipid basis and a 70% figure on a PS-specific basis are different products, and the basis on the COA must match the spec.
- **Method citation.** The identity and assay lines should name the method — a class-resolved HPLC procedure, an HPTLC identity method with a reference standard, or NMR. Uncited results cannot be reproduced or defended.
- **Phospholipid context.** If the spec defines companion-class limits, the COA spectrum should sit inside them; a PS assay that passes while the PC line sits outside its band is an internal contradiction worth chasing.
- **Identity line presence.** A COA without an identity result at all is incomplete for an ingredient whose value depends on being PS — assay, moisture and micro alone do not answer the identity question.
- **Coherence across lines.** The PS assay cannot exceed the total phospholipid figure; identity and assay methods should tell the same story about the same sample; test dates and lot references should form a plausible sequence.

### Red Flags That Warrant an Escalation

Some findings should stop the release decision rather than prompt a clarifying email:

- An assay reported on a total-phospholipid or unspecified basis for a grade sold on PS content.
- An identity line citing a method that cannot resolve PS from PC, such as a generic phosphorus test presented as identity.
- A phospholipid spectrum dominated by PC in material declared as converted PS — the signature of lecithin substitution.
- Refusal to provide chromatograms, HPTLC plate images or raw spectra with the report.
- Assays that swing sharply between lots of the same grade with no change notification.
- A price materially out of line with the declared grade and source.

Escalate through the quality agreement: hold the lot, keep retain samples sealed, commission independent testing with the method stated in advance, and compare results against the supplier's own lot history. Under 21 CFR Part 111 the identity-verification duty sits with the finished-goods company regardless of who ran the supplier's tests; EU buyers hold an equivalent due-diligence position under the general food-law principle that ingredients and their documentation must match what is placed on the market.

## FAQ

### Can a standard HPLC assay prove that a powder is phosphatidylserine?

Only if it resolves the phospholipid classes. A class-resolved HPLC method with ELSD or CAD detection quantifies PS specifically and reveals substitution; a UV-only or total-phosphorus method reads all classes together and can pass a powder that is mostly PC or lecithin. Ask which separation the method uses and request the chromatogram.

### Is HPTLC enough on its own to verify PS identity and purity?

HPTLC is a strong identity screen — the spot pattern is compared against a reference standard, and a substituted or diluted material usually shows a visibly wrong pattern. But it is semi-quantitative at best, so it confirms identity without certifying the assay figure. Pair it with a class-resolved HPLC assay.

### When is ³¹P NMR worth the extra cost?

When the stakes justify the strongest single method: first commercial lots from a new supplier, dispute testing, or any lot where HPLC and HPTLC disagree. NMR quantifies every phospholipid class in one spectrum, making substitution and dilution directly visible in the class ratios.

### What is the single most important thing to check on a PS certificate of analysis?

That the assay basis and identity method match the specification. PS as such is not total phospholipids, and an identity claim is only as good as the method behind it — a certificate stating a class-resolved method and a defined basis can be verified; one that does not cannot.

## Conclusion

Verifying genuine phosphatidylserine is a method-selection problem before it is a trust problem. A class-resolved HPLC assay answers the purity question — PS as such, not total phospholipids — HPTLC provides a fast identity screen, and ³¹P NMR delivers an orthogonal, substitution-proof class profile when the situation demands it. Judged against a spec with a defined assay basis and named methods, these tests turn a supplier's claims into evidence.
