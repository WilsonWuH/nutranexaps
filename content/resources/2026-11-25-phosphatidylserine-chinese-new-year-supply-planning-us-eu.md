## Introduction

Every winter, US and EU buyers of Asian-origin phosphatidylserine watch the same pattern repeat: orders placed casually in December arrive frustratingly late in March, and the explanation is rarely the supplier's production itself — it is the Lunar New Year shutdown stacked on top of normal lead times, holiday freight congestion and a QC backlog on restart. For 2027, Chinese New Year falls on Saturday, 6 February, with the public holiday running 6–12 February; most ingredient plants close for a longer window around it, and the operational effects begin weeks before the gates actually shut. Treated as a calendar curiosity, the shutdown quietly costs months. Treated as a planning event, it becomes a routine annual decision with a fixed checklist. This article walks through that checklist: what the window does to production, freight and QC, the ordering and stock decisions to make now, and the confirmations to secure from your supplier in writing. It starts from the material itself: the [bulk phosphatidylserine grades and specifications](/products/phosphatidylserine/) your shutdown plan has to cover.

## Main Content

### Why the Lunar New Year Shutdown Is a Planning Event, Not a Holiday

The official holiday lasts roughly a week, but the industrial reality is wider on both sides. Many ingredient factories wind down production one to two weeks before the holiday to allow cleaning, maintenance and stock take, and restart gradually afterwards as workers return — commonly over two to four weeks in total, varying by supplier, region and product line. For a phospholipid ingredient produced in batches, qualified lot by lot, and often packed with nitrogen flush and specific documentation, the consequences are specific: no new production slots during the window, no fresh certificates of analysis for batches that were not made, and a restart queue in which your order competes with every order other buyers deferred to February. The mistake most buyers make is treating the shutdown as the supplier's problem to absorb. Capacity is finite, and the buyers who communicate early are the ones whose batches get scheduled before the line stops. Understanding your supplier's normal production rhythm is the baseline for all of this, and the site's [production lead time and capacity planning buyer guide](/resources/phosphatidylserine-production-lead-time-capacity-planning-buyer-guide/) explains how to map it.

### How the Window Stretches Lead Times, Freight Capacity and QC Turnaround

Three bottlenecks compound during the same weeks. First, production: a batch confirmed in mid-January may be scheduled only if it beats the wind-down date; anything confirmed after it slips to late February, and since PS lots must be manufactured, tested and documented before packing, the slip is measured from batch release, not from the day the order was signed. Second, freight: export bookings surge ahead of the holiday as factories rush consignments out, which pushes ocean and air rates up and space down on major Asia-to-US and Asia-EU lanes; customs brokers and trucking also thin out during the holiday itself, so a container ready on paper can still sit. Third, QC and documentation: labs run at reduced staffing before and after the holiday, and third-party testing bookings behave like everything else — crowded on both sides of the window. The freight side of this squeeze is examined lane by lane in [the phosphatidylserine peak-season shipping and booking guide](/resources/phosphatidylserine-peak-season-shipping-booking-us-eu/); the planning point here is that all three bottlenecks interact, so a buyer who solves only the production slot can still lose the gain to a late booking.

### Ordering and Stock Decisions to Make Before the Window Closes

The practical plan has three layers. The first is timing: work backwards from the wind-down date your supplier declares — not from the holiday itself — and place the purchase order early enough that raw material procurement, batch scheduling and pre-shipment QC all fit inside December and January. The second is buffer stock: the honest question is how many weeks of PS inventory your formulations, filling plans and launch dates can tolerate without a top-up, and the shutdown stretches that number by the full window plus the restart queue; buyers who hold a post-holiday buffer sized to their real consumption avoid paying peak freight rates for a February emergency order. The third is specification discipline: a rushed pre-holiday order is where specification errors happen, so confirm assay concentration, source (soy or sunflower), packing format and document requirements at PO stage rather than during the wind-down week, when there is no time left to correct them. Sample-driven validation of a new grade should also be sequenced earlier in the quarter, because first-article evaluation that starts in late January can lose its restart window entirely.

### Communication and Documentation to Agree With Your Supplier

A shutdown plan is only as good as the written confirmations behind it. Before the factory closes, agree in writing: the last production and dispatch date the supplier commits to, and which date your PO is booked against; the batch schedule for your lot, with the certificate of analysis issued before packing rather than promised "after the holiday"; the packing and headspace conditions for the lot, since these are fixed at packing and cannot be retrofitted in February; the document package you need for clearance — certificate of analysis, safety data sheet, certificate of origin and any destination-specific declarations — issued while office staff are still in place; and a named contact for the shutdown period, because email silence for three weeks is not a plan, it is a risk. Ask when the supplier reopens and how the restart queue is prioritised, and put your restart order in before the break so it is already in the queue. None of this requires new commercial terms; it requires the normal terms confirmed against a calendar.

## FAQ

### Q: When should we place our phosphatidylserine order for the 2027 Lunar New Year window?

A: Anchor to the supplier's declared wind-down date rather than 6 February itself. As a working rhythm, POs confirmed by mid-December and production scheduled through January avoid most shutdown effects; confirmations landing after the wind-down typically wait for the full restart queue.

### Q: How much buffer stock should we carry through the shutdown?

A: Size it to your own consumption across the shutdown window plus the restart queue, not to a fixed rule. Cover your formulation and filling plan for the weeks you could realistically receive nothing, and remember a February emergency order buys at holiday-congested freight rates.

### Q: Does the shutdown affect PS quality, or only availability?

A: Primarily availability and timing. The batch was made under the same process whenever it was made; the quality risk lies in rushed decisions — accepting a lot without complete documentation, or packing changes agreed verbally during the wind-down. Keep normal acceptance discipline.

### Q: Should we book freight differently for pre-holiday shipments?

A: Yes. Export demand surges ahead of the holiday, so confirm booking space early, compare direct services against transshipment routings during the congested weeks, and decide whether air freight is justified for launch-critical lots that would otherwise miss the pre-holiday sailing.

## Conclusion

The Lunar New Year shutdown is the most predictable disruption in the Asian ingredient supply calendar, which makes it one of the cheapest to manage and the most expensive to ignore. The buyers who sail through it do three things: they anchor orders to the supplier's wind-down date instead of the holiday, they carry a buffer sized to their real consumption through the window, and they lock down batch schedules, packing conditions and document packages in writing before offices close. Do that once and the shutdown becomes a line item in the annual plan.
