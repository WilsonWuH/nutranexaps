## Introduction

Supplier selection for phosphatidylserine is usually decided on quality: specifications, COAs, audit results, and documentation. Those answers tell you whether a supplier can make PS. They do not tell you whether the supplier will still be making it in year three of your supply agreement — and with PS, that second question carries unusual weight.

![Financial analyst reviewing supplier financial documents and supply records in an office](/assets/images/phosphatidylserine-supplier-financial-stability-assessment-us-eu-financial-review.webp)

PS is a specialised phospholipid with limited production capacity, enzymatic conversion steps, and qualification cycles that run months from first sample to approved material. When a PS supplier fails, buyers do not simply switch orders — they lose a qualified material, restart requalification, and spend quarters explaining supply gaps to retail customers. This article sets out how to assess financial stability before signing, and which contract terms limit the damage if the assessment is later proven right.

## Main Content

## Why Financial Stability Matters More for Specialised Ingredients

Commodity ingredients have deep supplier markets: if one vendor fails, procurement re-quotes and ships within weeks. Specialised ingredients like PS behave differently on three dimensions. Capacity is concentrated — the number of plants producing enzymatically converted PS at food or GMP grade is small, so a failure removes a large share of realistic alternates. Qualification is slow — your approved-supplier file, customer approvals, and any retail-audit recognition attach to a specific manufacturing site, and all of it must be rebuilt after a switch. Working capital is heavy — phospholipid production ties cash in raw materials, long campaigns, and inventory held against your forecast, so a supplier stretched thin cuts corners precisely where your quality system cares most: maintenance, QC staffing, and insurance.

The practical conclusion: for PS, financial due diligence is not a finance department formality. It is supply-chain continuity work, and it belongs in the same qualification file as the audit checklist.

## Red Flags in Behaviour, Paperwork, and the Supply Chain

Financial distress rarely announces itself in a sales call. It shows in patterns. Commercial behaviour: aggressive discounting to win volume cash, requests for large prepayments or shortened payment terms, sudden key-account manager turnover, and evasive answers about capacity or campaign scheduling. Paperwork: reluctance to name the actual manufacturing site, inconsistent legal entity names between contracts, invoices, and COAs, expired or mismatched certifications, and quality documents that arrive late or edited. Supply-chain signals: rising order lead times, partial shipments justified as logistics issues, substitution of packaging or carrier materials without notification, and third-party lab results that drift from the supplier's COA.

None of these is proof of distress in isolation — some are ordinary supplier behaviour. The pattern across several signals, over two or three quarters, is what warrants a formal review before you extend term length or volume commitments.

## Financial Due Diligence Steps for Cross-Border Suppliers

Cross-border diligence is harder than domestic, but a structured pass gets most of the signal. First, establish the legal entity: registered name, ownership structure, and years trading, verified through the local business registry rather than the supplier's website. Second, use commercial credit reports — international providers cover major manufacturing regions and rate payment behaviour, legal filings, and risk scores. Third, request financial statements where the supplier will share them; mid-size private manufacturers often provide audited or management accounts under NDA, and liquidity ratios, debt service coverage, and receivables ageing tell you how the balance sheet is breathing. Fourth, check litigation and enforcement records through public court and registry sources. Fifth, weigh what the site visit shows: a well-maintained plant with stable staffing and real inventory contradicts the distress narrative faster than any spreadsheet confirms it.

Calibrate depth to exposure. A dual-sourced spot relationship tolerates lighter diligence than a single-sourced three-year agreement with exclusivity. Put the effort where the switching cost lives.

## Contract Protections That Survive a Supplier Failure

Diligence reduces the probability of failure; contract terms reduce its cost. The protections that matter most in ingredient supply agreements are concrete. Financial security: performance bonds or bank guarantees sized to cover requalification cost, not just order value. Payment discipline on your side: pay against documents rather than large prepayments, and never prepay a distressed supplier for capacity you have not reserved in writing. Inventory and ownership: consignment or safety-stock agreements with clear title transfer terms, so stock you funded is legally yours before insolvency proceedings freeze assets. Dual-sourcing architecture: contractual volume splits that keep a qualified second source warm, accepting a modest premium as the price of optionality. Step-in and continuity rights: tooling, artwork, and process-documentation ownership that lets a replacement manufacturer take over; transition-assistance obligations; and escrowed critical documentation where appropriate. Termination and notice triggers tied to objective events — ownership change, loss of certifications, repeated late delivery — so exit does not require proving insolvency after the fact.

## Monitoring After Signature

Financial stability is a snapshot, not a property. Long-term agreements should include monitoring: annual refresh of credit reports, certification validity checks, on-time-delivery and quality-metrics review each quarter, and a standing trigger for re-diligence when pre-agreed signals appear. Suppliers who are stable welcome this as routine supplier management; the reaction to a reasonable monitoring request is itself a signal.

## FAQ

### Q: Our supplier passed a quality audit. Is financial diligence really necessary?

A: Yes — they measure different things. An audit assesses manufacturing capability at a point in time; financial diligence assesses whether the organisation behind the plant will still operate it profitably across your agreement term. For specialised ingredients like PS, losing the supplier means losing the qualified material, so viability deserves its own file section.

### Q: What is the single most useful financial metric for an overseas ingredient supplier?

A: Liquidity trend, not a single-year figure. Two to three years of current ratio, cash position, and receivables ageing shows whether working capital is strengthening or degrading. Phospholipid production is working-capital intensive, so liquidity erosion is the earliest quantitative distress signal most buyers can access.

### Q: Can we include financial covenants in a supply agreement?

A: You can include notification triggers — ownership change, loss of certification, material litigation — more easily than audited financial covenants, which private manufacturers rarely accept. Notification triggers plus objective termination rights give you most of the practical protection.

### Q: How much safety stock is reasonable for a single-sourced PS supply?

A: Commonly one to three months of demand, sized to your requalification timeline rather than to comfort. If qualifying an alternate PS source takes two quarters, your coverage should bridge that period — and the honest fix is warming a second source, not endless stock.

## Conclusion

For a specialised ingredient like phosphatidylserine, the cheapest insurance in a long-term supply agreement is a financial-viability assessment done before signature and a contract that prices failure realistically: security sized to requalification, payment discipline, warm second sources, and exit triggers that do not require an insolvency filing to exercise. Buyers who treat financial stability as part of supplier qualification — not a finance afterthought — avoid the scenario where quality was perfect and supply still disappeared.
