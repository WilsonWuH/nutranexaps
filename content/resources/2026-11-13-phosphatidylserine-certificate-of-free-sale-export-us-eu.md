## Introduction

Sooner or later, most phosphatidylserine (PS) buyers exporting into Latin America, the Gulf, Southeast Asia or Africa hit the same wall: the registration dossier or customs file asks for a certificate of free sale (CFS) for the ingredient, issued by an authority in the country of origin, often notarized, apostilled or consular-legalized on top. Unlike a COA, it says nothing about the batch in front of you — it is a government-endorsed statement that the product is lawfully marketed at home, and it is routinely underestimated at quotation. This article walks through what a CFS actually attests, which authorities issue it for PS powder, when legalization is required, and the wording traps that cause rejections. The starting point is knowing exactly which product the certificate will describe, so [review the phosphatidylserine grades and specification detail](/products/phosphatidylserine/) before commissioning the document.

## Main Content

### What a Certificate of Free Sale Is — and What It Is Not

A certificate of free sale is an attestation, issued by a competent authority in the exporting country, that a named product is freely sold in — or lawfully manufactured for export from — that country. It typically lists the product name, the manufacturer, and sometimes the packer or exporter, and it is signed, stamped and often notarized. Many import jurisdictions treat it as evidence that the product is not a rogue import: if the ingredient is on sale in the US or an EU member state, local regulators presumably have oversight over its production.

What it is not is equally important. A CFS makes no statement about the quality of a specific lot, its assay, or its contaminants — that remains the COA's job. It does not replace a certificate of analysis, a certificate of origin, or GMP certifications, and no authority inspects the goods before signing it. Buyers sometimes assume the FDA issues free sale certificates for supplement ingredients; it does not — the agency only permits certain attestations by third parties, which is why PS buyers must understand the alternative issuing routes below. Treating the CFS as a formality to request "later" is precisely how registration timelines slip.

### Which Markets Require a CFS for PS Ingredients

The CFS requirement clusters in markets where supplement or functional-food ingredients pass through a registration or notification step. Health authorities in the Gulf (through SFDA and GCC systems), Mexico's COFEPRIS, ASEAN regulators, and several Latin American and African authorities ask importers to attach a free sale certificate from the country of manufacture — usually legalized — as part of the ingredient dossier. Customs authorities in some of these markets also request it independently at clearance, particularly for first shipments from a new supplier.

The practical nuance is that the same PS shipment may need the certificate at two different layers: the finished-product marketer needs it for its product registration, and the ingredient importer may need a separate CFS naming the raw material and its manufacturer. A certificate issued for a branded 50%-PS finished powder does not automatically cover bulk powder sold to another buyer, and a CFS naming one manufacturing site does not cover material from a sister plant. Map the requirement against your specific route — ingredient-level or finished-goods-level, and which legal entity is named — before ordering the document, and confirm with your importer of record which version the authority accepts.

### Who Issues the CFS: US and EU Issuing Authorities

In the **United States**, the recognized routes are state-level agencies — departments of health, agriculture or commerce, depending on the state — and the U.S. Commercial Service's certificate of free sale program, which attests that the product is manufactured and marketed in the US. Most require the applicant to demonstrate that the product is on sale: invoices, a listing, or website evidence, plus notarized company attestation. Turnaround ranges from same-week to several weeks depending on the state and the legalization that follows.

In the **European Union**, free sale attestations for supplements and food ingredients are typically issued by the competent authority of the member state where the product is marketed, or by chambers of commerce acting as certifying bodies. The authority will want evidence of lawful placing on the market in that member state — which means the supplier's EU entity or importer needs an established, documented presence there. When you qualify a PS supplier for registration-driven markets, make the CFS route a qualification question: which authority issues their certificates, how often they request them, and how long the full legalized chain has taken historically. A supplier who has never issued one will learn on your timeline, not theirs.

### Apostille vs Consular Legalization: Getting the CFS Accepted

The certificate itself is only half the work — most receiving authorities do not trust a foreign government stamp without an authentication layer on top. If both the issuing and receiving countries are parties to the Hague Apostille Convention, an apostille certificate attached by the designated competent authority in the issuing country is usually sufficient. If the destination is not a Hague party — several Gulf and Middle Eastern markets among them — the document must pass consular legalization: notarization, authentication by the issuing state or foreign ministry, then certification at the destination country's embassy or consulate, each step with its own fee and queue.

Legalization lead time is the classic surprise. Consular chains routinely take three to six weeks, and embassy holidays, staffing changes or document errors restart the clock. Budget it into the registration calendar explicitly, in parallel with dossier preparation rather than after it. Note that the same authentication logic applies to other origin documents in the pack — [our review of the certificate of origin for PS shipments](/resources/phosphatidylserine-certificate-of-origin-review-us-eu/) covers the chamber-of-commerce and legalization workflow that a CFS will follow closely.

### Wording, Scope and Common Rejection Traps

Rejections on CFS documents rarely come from the certificate's existence — they come from what it says. The recurring traps:

- **Name mismatch.** The product name, manufacturer legal name and site address on the CFS must match the registration dossier and the commercial documents exactly. "Nutranexa LLC" versus "Nutranexa" or a renamed production site is enough for a reviewer to bounce the file.
- **Scope too narrow or too broad.** A certificate listing one grade (for example, 50% PS powder) will not cover a 70% grade or a sunflower-derived variant. Conversely, authorities in some markets reject certificates that describe vague families of products. Decide whether you need one certificate per grade or a single multi-line certificate, and confirm the authority accepts that format.
- **"Freely sold" vs "freely manufactured for export".** If your supplier sells the PS powder only B2B and it is not retail-marketed, the correct attestation may be lawful manufacture for export rather than free sale in the domestic market. Issuing authorities support both, but the wrong wording sends the dossier back.
- **Stale documents.** Many authorities impose a validity window — commonly six to twelve months from issue. A certificate ordered "whenever" during qualification may be expired by the time the registration is reviewed.

### Refresh Cycles and Keeping the CFS Current

Because validity windows are short relative to registration timelines, CFS management is a recurring task, not a one-off. Ask your supplier how they handle refresh cycles: whether they hold a standing arrangement with the issuing authority, whether they track expiry dates for regular customers, and whether a new certificate is triggered automatically when the manufacturing site, legal entity or product name changes. Fold the answer into your supplier documentation audit — the same discipline that governs [how a PS specification and COA package is reviewed](/resources/phosphatidylserine-coa-specification-certificate-review/) should extend to export attestations, since a CFS that names a discontinued grade or an old site undermines the consistency of the whole document pack.

## FAQ

### Q: Does the FDA issue a certificate of free sale for phosphatidylserine powder?

A: No. The FDA does not certify products for export. In the US, CFS documents come from state-level agencies or the U.S. Commercial Service program, backed by evidence that the product is on sale.

### Q: Can one certificate of free sale cover both soy-derived and sunflower-derived PS?

A: Only if the certificate explicitly lists both products and the issuing authority accepts a multi-item format. Many authorities issue per-product certificates, so confirm the destination market's preference before requesting the document.

### Q: How long does consular legalization of a CFS take?

A: Typically three to six weeks end to end, depending on the embassy, with notarization and foreign-ministry authentication adding further days; complex routes that cross jurisdictions take longer.

### Q: Who pays for the apostille or legalization?

A: It is negotiated: some suppliers include a stated number of legalized certificates annually in their commercial terms, others pass through fees. Agree ownership at quotation stage — the costs are small, but an unclear owner leads to delays.

## Conclusion

The certificate of free sale is a modest document with outsized scheduling power: it sits at the front of registration dossiers, moves on government timelines no buyer controls, and rejects over details as small as a legal-name suffix or a validity window. Build it into the sourcing conversation early — ask which authority issues the supplier's certificates, what the legalized chain costs in time, and how refreshes are managed — and the CFS becomes routine rather than a bottleneck.
