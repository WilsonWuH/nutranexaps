## Introduction

Ingredient quotations for phosphatidylserine arrive through two very different channels: direct from factories that run the enzymatic conversion themselves, and through trading companies that resell someone else's output under their own paperwork. Both can be legitimate counterparties, but they carry different risks, different documentation chains and different leverage in a dispute — and many buyers discover which one they have been dealing with only when something goes wrong. Verifying supplier identity before the first purchase order is one of the cheapest quality controls in an ingredient programme. This article sets out how US and EU buyers can tell a true phosphatidylserine manufacturer from a reseller, which documents carry evidential weight, what to ask during onboarding, and how to contract when a trader is acceptable. ## Main Content

### Why the Manufacturer Question Shapes Your Sourcing File

A manufacturing claim affects almost every downstream control in an ingredient programme. When the supplier is the factory, change control is direct: a raw-material switch or process change reaches you from the people who made the decision. When the supplier is a trader, that same information travels through an extra link, and each link is a place where notification can be delayed, filtered or lost. Audits behave the same way — an audit invitation from a factory opens production records; an invitation from a trader opens a meeting room.

The distinction also drives regulatory responsibility. Under US dietary supplement GMPs, the buyer is responsible for supplier qualification, and under FSMA the importer holds foreign-supplier verification obligations regardless of who physically produced the material. In the EU, traceability one step back is far easier when that step is the production site itself.

### The Commercial Risks Hidden Behind an Undeclared Middleman

None of these risks make traders illegitimate; they make *undeclared* middlemen expensive. The first risk is margin stacking: a trader selling factory output at a markup has no obligation to disclose the underlying cost, so price benchmarks and negotiation move against you. The second is mixed lots — a reseller without dedicated inventory may consolidate material from several factories into one delivery, breaking lot traceability exactly when a COA needs to defend a batch. The third is continuity: if the trader's upstream relationship ends, your supply stops with no notice and no pathway to the plant.

Documentation integrity is the fourth and most serious risk. COAs, allergen statements and regulatory declarations should originate at the production site; a reseller who re-issues documents under their own letterhead introduces transcription, version and accountability problems that surface during audits, customs review or complaint investigations. Buyers who qualify a "supplier" without knowing which legal entity actually manufactures inherit all four risks silently.

### Document Evidence That Identifies a True PS Manufacturer

Documents do the work that claims cannot. Start with regulatory registrations: a US food facility registration number tied to the production address, and for EU-facing suppliers, an approved-establishments listing in the relevant member state. A registration held in the name of the quoting entity at the *production* address is strong evidence; one under a different name, or not tied to a site, is not. Third-party certification certificates (FSSC 22000, BRCGS, ISO 22000) are equally revealing because the certified scope names the exact site and the processes covered — check that the scope covers enzymatic phospholipid conversion, not only warehousing or trading.

Lot-level paperwork adds the next layer. A manufacturer's COA carries the production site's details and matches the lot numbers printed on the packaging and drum labels. Cross-check the COA against the SDS and the allergen statement: same legal entity, same site, consistent contact details. Mismatched letterheads, lot numbers that do not appear on drum labels, or COAs with re-issued formatting are classic reseller fingerprints. Finally, ask for the batch manufacturing record extract or a process flow diagram for the actual production step — factories produce these readily; intermediaries stall. For the broader onboarding context these documents feed, [our phosphatidylserine supplier audit checklist](/resources/phosphatidylserine-supplier-audit-checklist-us-eu/) shows how to structure the same evidence into a formal qualification file.

### Questions to Ask During Supplier Onboarding

Verification is mostly a conversation, and four questions sort counterparties quickly. First: "At which address is the phosphatidylserine produced, and who holds the registration for that site?" A direct answer names a factory; a vague answer about "our group" deserves follow-up. Second: "Will you host the audit at the production site, and when?" Genuine manufacturers schedule audits without friction; traders propose office meetings or introduce a "partner factory" you have never seen in the document set. Third: "Is every COA issued at the production site, and may we verify one directly with the issuing laboratory?" Fourth: "Which certification scope covers the conversion step, and can you send the certificate with its scope annex?"

Ask the same questions of several counterparties and the patterns become obvious. Note that evasive answers are themselves data — a supplier who cannot describe its own enzyme-conversion line in one paragraph is not running one. To test registration claims independently, US buyers can cross-reference the facility registration details their broker pulls on import, and [our review of FDA food facility registration for ingredient suppliers](/resources/phosphatidylserine-fda-food-facility-registration-us/) explains what a verifiable registration actually contains.

### When a Trader Is the Right Counterparty — and How to Contract for It

None of this means traders should be disqualified automatically. Trading companies add value when they consolidate volumes from multiple qualified factories, hold local stock that shortens lead times, or bridge language and payment barriers on smaller orders. The problem is not the model; it is the concealment. A good trader discloses the manufacturing site, passes the audit through to it, and delivers factory-issued documents unaltered.

Contracting rules follow from disclosure. Name the production site and its certification numbers in the quality agreement. Require change notifications to flow from the manufacturing site, not be relayed. Require lot numbers, packaging and labels to be traceable to a single named factory per shipment, and reserve the right to audit that factory. With those terms in place, a disclosed trader can serve a programme nearly as well as a direct relationship — and often faster.

### A Verification Checklist Before the First Order

Before issuing a purchase order, confirm five points: the legal entity you are contracting with and its role in the supply chain; the production address and the registration or certification held there, with scope annexes that cover the conversion step; consistency of entity name, site and contact details across COA, SDS, allergen statement and drum labels; willingness to host an audit at the production site; and, when a trader is involved, a quality agreement that names the factory and preserves factory-issued documentation. A supplier who clears all five will clear audits, complaints and change control the same way — and a supplier who fails one of them has told you what your qualification file will look like before you have paid anything.

## FAQ

### Q: Why does it matter if my phosphatidylserine comes from a trading company?

A: A disclosed trader can be workable, but an undeclared one stacks margin, risks mixing lots from several factories, and inserts a relay between you and change notifications. Every downstream control becomes harder when you do not know who produced the material.

### Q: What is the strongest single piece of evidence that a supplier is the manufacturer?

A: Third-party certification with a scope annex naming the production site and covering the enzymatic phospholipid conversion process. Scope documents are issued by certification bodies against a specific site, so they are difficult for a reseller to present falsely — and they cross-check cleanly against facility registrations and drum labels.

### Q: Should I refuse to buy from traders altogether?

A: No. Traders can offer stock availability, consolidated volumes and commercial convenience, especially for smaller programmes. Accept them with conditions: named production sites in the quality agreement, factory-issued documents, single-factory traceability per shipment and audit rights at the plant.

### Q: Can I verify a supplier's US facility registration myself?

A: You can verify the registration number as part of import filings and supplier qualification, reconciling it against the production address on COAs and certifications. Registration alone is not proof of manufacturing — it confirms a registered facility, not what happens inside it, so combine it with scope certifications and lot-level document checks.

## Conclusion

Supplier identity is the foundation every other qualification step stands on. Establish early whether your phosphatidylserine counterparty is the factory or a reseller, demand the documents that prove it — registrations and certification scopes tied to the production site, lot-consistent COAs and unaltered declarations — and write disclosure obligations into the quality agreement when a trader is in the chain. Verification costs a few emails; its absence can cost a batch, an audit or a supply line.
