## Why TSE/BSE Statement Review Is Its Own Buyer Task

### EU TSE controls focus on animal-origin risk, not casual product nicknames

The official EU sources frame TSE/BSE controls around **animal-origin risk**. The Commission’s TSE page explains that Regulation (EC) No 999/2001 is the legal basis for almost all legislative actions on TSEs and that the rules cover trade in live animals and products of animal origin. The Commission’s page on products of animal origin for human consumption separately lists Regulation (EC) No 999/2001 among the public-health requirements that apply to entry into the Union for those products. Another Commission page on official controls states that imported products of animal origin present a high level of risk and therefore undergo specific checks.

For phosphatidylserine buyers, that does not mean every route automatically falls into an animal-origin import framework. It means the buyer should stop answering TSE/BSE questions with a nickname such as "PS powder" or "sunflower route" and start answering them with a controlled route-specific document. If the commercial file is presenting a route as plant-derived, the statement should make that route clear enough that procurement, QA, and customer-facing teams are not forced to infer the answer.

The business lesson is simple: a TSE/BSE statement is not usually requested because the buyer believes phosphatidylserine is an animal-origin product. It is requested because the buyer needs a formal answer showing how the route should be classified and reviewed inside the approval file.

### Plant-derived commercial routes still need controlled document answers

This is where many teams become careless. They correctly understand that a soy-derived or sunflower-derived route is not the same thing as a bovine or ovine product. Then they assume no further statement control is needed.

That shortcut causes downstream friction because European approval files often ask a more exact question:

| Buyer question | What the team is really trying to confirm |
| --- | --- |
| Do you have a TSE/BSE-free statement? | Is the quoted route documented clearly enough for QA or customer use? |
| Is this route animal-origin free? | Does the file match the plant-derived route we are actually approving? |
| Can the customer use this declaration in its technical pack? | Is the statement current, route-specific, and controlled enough for this stage? |
| Is a COA enough? | No, because a COA is not the same as a route-specific animal-origin declaration |

That is why TSE/BSE review belongs beside, not inside, the other statement reviews. [Allergen statement review](/resources/phosphatidylserine-allergen-statement-us-eu/) answers a source and allergen-control question. [GMO statement review](/resources/phosphatidylserine-gmo-statement-us-eu/) answers a traceability and declaration question. [Novel-food review for EU buyers](/resources/phosphatidylserine-novel-food-review-europe/) answers a route authorization question. TSE/BSE review answers a narrower but separate question: **how should the animal-origin risk position for this exact phosphatidylserine route be documented?**

## The Six-Part Buyer Workflow for Phosphatidylserine TSE/BSE Review

### Lock the exact phosphatidylserine route and commercial stage first

Before requesting or approving any TSE/BSE statement, fix the commercial route in writing. Is the team reviewing general phosphatidylserine as an early comparison, or one source-specific route such as soy PS or sunflower PS? Is the statement needed for shortlist qualification, quotation approval, contract-manufacturer onboarding, distributor resale, or first bulk order?

This matters because TSE/BSE questions quickly become vague when the route is moving. A salesperson may send a general PS document. QA may be reviewing a soy-route file. The customer may be asking about sunflower because the non-soy position is commercially attractive. If the route is not fixed first, the statement review becomes a filing exercise instead of a control exercise.

The opening note should usually record:

- exact product route
- intended Europe-facing use
- commercial stage
- documents already in hand
- specific TSE/BSE or animal-origin question still open

That note prevents the team from asking for one statement and applying it to several different route discussions.

### Separate route identity from the customer’s animal-origin concern

The second step is to identify what the downstream party is actually asking.

Sometimes the customer wants a simple animal-origin declaration for supplier qualification. Sometimes QA needs a controlled TSE/BSE-free statement in the technical file. Sometimes a contract manufacturer wants confirmation that the route under review is plant-derived before it aligns the broader compliance pack.

Those are related questions, but they are not identical. The buyer should separate:

1. the **route** being quoted
2. the **document type** being requested
3. the **commercial stage** at which the answer will be used

That separation keeps the review practical. A shortlist-stage distributor may only need confirmation that the supplier can issue the right route-specific statement. A first-order or customer-dossier stage may require a current formal statement plus matching source and specification files. Without that distinction, teams overpromise early and underdocument later.

### Review the TSE/BSE statement, specification, and source file together

This is the control point that saves time later. Buyers should not review the TSE/BSE statement in isolation. They should compare it directly against the route description, current specification, and related source file.

At minimum, the review should ask:

| Checkpoint | Buyer question |
| --- | --- |
| Route wording | Does the statement clearly belong to soy PS, sunflower PS, or only a general PS discussion? |
| Statement scope | Is it addressing TSE/BSE, animal-origin status, or both? |
| Specification match | Does the current specification describe the same route as the statement? |
| Supporting file path | Is there a source declaration or related document that helps explain the route being stated? |
| Commercial stage | Is this statement enough for shortlist review, or is a more formal controlled file required now? |

That comparison matters because a TSE/BSE statement often fails for one simple reason: it belongs to a broader product family while the commercial file has already narrowed into a specific route. A buyer should not assume the route match is obvious. It should be checked deliberately.

### Escalate route or process questions before they become customer promises

Not every TSE/BSE question can be closed at procurement level. Some should be escalated immediately.

Escalation is usually appropriate when:

- the commercial route changed after the original statement was collected
- the statement is generic while the customer is asking for a route-specific answer
- the file does not clearly distinguish between a general plant-source claim and a formal TSE/BSE declaration
- the buyer is being asked to answer process or material questions not covered by the current controlled file
- a downstream customer wants to reuse the statement in a way that exceeds the current approval stage

The right escalation note does not need to be dramatic. It should simply state:

1. which route is under review
2. what question remains open
3. which file is missing or unclear
4. who owns the next answer

That is far safer than letting sales or procurement translate a generic statement into a more specific customer promise than the file actually supports.

### Keep TSE/BSE review separate from novel-food, allergen, and GMO files

One reason these files drift is that teams treat every compliance question as if it were interchangeable. It is not.

| File type | What it helps answer | What it does not replace |
| --- | --- | --- |
| TSE/BSE statement | How the route’s animal-origin risk position is documented | Novel-food route confirmation |
| Allergen statement | How source and allergen handling should be reviewed | TSE/BSE or animal-origin scope |
| GMO statement | How GMO-related source and declaration questions are handled | TSE/BSE scope |
| COA | How a batch or reference sample is reported | Controlled animal-origin declaration |
| Specification | How the route is technically described | A current route-specific TSE/BSE statement |

That separation is commercially useful because it prevents a customer questionnaire from being answered with whichever file happens to be easiest to find. The stronger workflow is to map each question to one controlled document type, then close it clearly.

### Close with a go, hold, or escalation decision

The last step is to record an outcome another team can use:

- **Go:** the route, statement, and supporting file are aligned well enough for the current stage.
- **Hold:** the route may still be commercially attractive, but the current statement or route match is not controlled enough yet.
- **Escalate:** the question goes beyond the current file and needs technical, quality, or customer-specific review.

This final note matters because TSE/BSE questions often resurface late. The quote is moving, the customer wants a document pack, and QA realizes nobody wrote down whether the current statement actually belongs to the route under approval. One short closeout note prevents that avoidable loop.

## Common Mistakes in Phosphatidylserine TSE/BSE Review

Most failures come from file-control mistakes rather than from the existence of the question itself.

1. Asking for a general TSE/BSE statement before the team has fixed whether it is approving soy PS, sunflower PS, or only a general PS benchmark.
2. Treating a plant-source product nickname as if it automatically answers a controlled animal-origin question.
3. Using a COA or product page in place of a current route-specific statement.
4. Letting a customer questionnaire turn a generic statement into a more specific promise than the controlled file supports.
5. Mixing TSE/BSE review with allergen, GMO, or novel-food files and assuming one of them can stand in for the others.
6. Closing the commercial file without recording a go, hold, or escalation decision.

The practical fix is narrow and repeatable: keep one question at the center of the review. **What controlled TSE/BSE or animal-origin statement do we need for this exact phosphatidylserine route at this exact Europe-facing stage?**
