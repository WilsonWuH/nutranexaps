## Introduction

A phosphatidylserine lot can leave the factory with a clean peroxide value and arrive three ocean weeks later with a number that no longer matches the certificate. Nothing leaked, nothing broke, no documents are missing — the powder simply spent a month inside a steel container crossing the Gulf in July or holding at a tropical transshipment port, and phosphatidylserine is an oxidatively sensitive phospholipid that treats that heat as a processing step. US and EU buyers usually manage peroxide value as a specification number; hot-climate lanes turn it into a logistics variable, governed by packing, routing and season as much as by the batch itself. This article explains what sustained container heat does to PS in transit, the packing and lane controls worth writing into the purchase order, and how to test and judge a lot on arrival. It starts from the material itself: the [bulk phosphatidylserine grades and stability specifications](/products/phosphatidylserine/) your transit plan has to protect.

## Main Content

### Why Transit Heat Is a Quality Variable, Not Just a Comfort Problem

Phosphatidylserine is produced from phospholipid fractions that remain vulnerable to the same chemistry as any fat system: oxygen attacking unsaturated fatty acid chains, with peroxide value as the standard early indicator of that attack. The reason transit deserves its own attention is scale. A container standing on an open terminal deck in a Gulf or South Asian summer can reach internal temperatures of 50–60 °C during the day and stay warm overnight, and a typical Asia-to-US or Asia-to-EU routing spends three to six weeks in that environment once loading, ocean legs, transshipment dwell and inland carriage are counted. Oxidation reactions accelerate steeply with every additional ten degrees, so a lane that looks routine on a map can deliver more oxidative stress to the powder than the supplier's entire production and packing process. This is not a specification failure — the specification was met at packing. It is an unmanaged process step sitting between the certificate of analysis and your receiving dock, and it is a step the buyer partly controls through what is ordered, how it is packed and how it ships.

### What Happens to Peroxide Value Inside a Hot Container

Three variables decide how far peroxide value drifts on a hot lane. The first is headspace oxygen: a drum or box that was nitrogen-flushed carries far less oxygen to react than one packed in air, so the flush declared on the specification is not a formality — it is the primary oxygen supply limit for the whole transit. The second is the initial condition of the powder: a batch packed at a POV near the upper end of its limit has less margin than one packed low, which is why buyers benefit from asking for typical batch POV values, not just the limit itself. The third is the antioxidant system: tocopherols in the grade are consumed as they work, and a long hot transit spends that reserve faster than the shelf-life model assumed. One subtlety matters for interpretation: peroxides are intermediates, not end products, so POV can rise and then partially fall as peroxides break down into secondary oxidation products — a lot can look stable on POV while secondary markers have moved. How POV is defined, measured and set as a limit is covered in [the phosphatidylserine peroxide value and oxidative stability review](/resources/phosphatidylserine-oxidative-stability-peroxide-value-us-eu/).

### Packing and Lane Controls Worth Writing Into the Purchase Order

Transit heat is managed with two levers the buyer influences directly: what the powder is packed in and how it is routed. On packing, require the headspace condition (nitrogen flush, with a residual oxygen declaration if the supplier can measure it), the packaging barrier — a foil-laminate liner behaves very differently from plain polyethylene over six hot weeks — desiccant where the format allows it, and the antioxidant system stated on the specification rather than in marketing copy, because the type and level of tocopherol protection is a genuine variable on long lanes. The antioxidant side of that equation is compared in [the phosphatidylserine antioxidant system and tocopherol specification article](/resources/phosphatidylserine-antioxidant-system-tocopherol-spec-us-eu/). On routing, the practical controls are less exotic than they sound: prefer direct services over routings with long tropical transshipment dwell, avoid summer bookings on lanes where the powder has historically drifted, ask whether a temperature-controlled container at an ambient setpoint is realistic for your order size, and for high-value or launch-critical lots consider air freight despite the cost. Container-level loading practice and booking seasonality are covered in the site's container loading and peak-season guides; what matters here is that lane choice is a quality decision, not only a freight-rate decision, and it belongs in the same purchase order as the packing clause.

### Arrival Testing and Judging a POV-Drifted Lot

The receiving decision should be defined before the first hot-lane order, not improvised when a number comes back high. Start by testing the arrival sample with the same method used on the dispatch certificate, because method differences alone can explain apparent drift. Compare against the specification limit, but also track arrival POV against dispatch POV as a trend: a lot at 3 that rose from 1 may be conforming yet worth a conversation, while a flat trend across seasons says the packing system is working. Set an internal alert level below the specification limit, so a drift pattern triggers a packing or routing review before a lot ever fails outright. When a lot does exceed the limit on arrival, POV rarely tells the whole story: peroxide value captures early-stage oxidation only, so confirm with secondary markers such as anisidine value or sensory assessment before deciding to reject, and retain logger or carrier temperature data where the lane was instrumented so the cause is auditable on both sides. Every drifted lot should feed the next order: a tighter oxygen declaration, a different liner, a different season or a different lane.

## FAQ

### Q: Does a POV rise in transit automatically make the lot non-conforming?

A: No. The specification limit is the conformance line, and testing is done on the arrival sample against that limit. A rise within limits is a trend signal, not a rejection reason — but a repeated pattern should trigger a packing or lane review before it becomes one.

### Q: Should we require a temperature data logger in every PS shipment?

A: Not every shipment. Loggers on the first shipments of a new lane or a new packing format establish the time-temperature profile you are actually buying; after that, periodic or high-risk-season logging usually gives the same confidence at lower cost and fewer documentation loops at clearance.

### Q: Is air freight worth it for heat-sensitive phosphatidylserine orders?

A: For small, high-value or launch-critical lots crossing hot lanes in summer, often yes — transit time collapses from weeks to days, and the pallet environment is milder than a steel container on a terminal deck. For routine full-container volumes, packing and routing controls are usually the economical answer.

### Q: Is soy-derived or sunflower-derived PS more heat-tolerant in transit?

A: The fatty acid profile matters more than the botanical source, and both soy-derived and sunflower-derived grades carry unsaturated chains that oxidise under heat. Choose the source for allergen, label and market reasons, and manage transit heat with packing, antioxidant declaration and lane choice for either.

## Conclusion

Hot-climate shipping does not create a new quality problem for phosphatidylserine — it moves part of the oxidation timeline out of the factory and into your logistics plan. The buyers who keep POV stable through Gulf summers and tropical transshipments treat the lane as a process step: headspace and barrier controls written into the order, antioxidant protection declared, seasons and routings chosen deliberately, and arrival testing defined with alert levels before the first container books. Build that plan once and peroxide value stops being a surprise on the receiving dock.
