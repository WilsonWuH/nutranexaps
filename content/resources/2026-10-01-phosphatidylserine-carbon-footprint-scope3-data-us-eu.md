## Introduction

Somewhere in the last two reporting cycles, "ask the supplier for carbon data" moved from the sustainability team's wishlist to the procurement manager's task list. For US brands it arrives through large customers' supplier questionnaires; for EU brands it arrives harder, through corporate sustainability reporting obligations that make purchased goods the largest, least-known slice of the emissions inventory.

![Procurement manager and sustainability analyst reviewing supplier carbon footprint data on a spreadsheet in an office](/assets/images/resource-ps-phosphatidylserine-carbon-footprint-scope3-data-us-eu-main.webp)

Phosphatidylserine sits in an awkward spot for this exercise: a specialized lipid made through enzyme-driven processing, whose footprint depends heavily on the upstream crop, the energy mix of the plant and the allocation choices buried in the calculation. Buyers who request "the carbon footprint" receive answers they cannot compare; buyers who request the right set of numbers receive data they can defend to auditors and customers. This article defines that request.

## Main Content

## Why Carbon Data Now Reaches the Ingredient Buyer

Under the GHG Protocol, reported emissions divide into Scopes: Scope 1 covers a company's own sources, Scope 2 its purchased energy, and Scope 3 everything in the value chain. For most food and supplement brands, Scope 3 Category 1 — purchased goods and services — is by far the largest slice, commonly 70–90% of the total footprint. An ingredient like PS is a small line in the P&L but a real line in the inventory, and the only way to make that line accurate is supplier data rather than industry-average factors.

The pull comes from three directions at once: EU-based brands and many large US brands report under frameworks that increasingly demand value-chain figures, enterprise customers push supplier ESG questionnaires down their chains, and retailers apply their own scorecards. The buyer's question is therefore no longer whether to collect supplier carbon data, but which data is worth collecting and how to tell good numbers from decorative ones.

## The Data to Request from a PS Supplier

Ask for a short, standardized pack so answers arrive comparable across suppliers and across your other ingredients:

- Product carbon footprint per functional unit: kg CO2e per kg of phosphatidylserine, stated for a defined grade and concentration (for example, 50% PS powder), cradle-to-gate.
- Boundary statement: which life cycle stages are included — crop cultivation, oil extraction, enzyme conversion, spray drying, packaging — and which are excluded, plus the study year.
- Allocation method: how burdens are divided among co-products (mass, economic or system expansion). For a lipid made from lecithin fractions, this single choice can move the number more than any real process difference.
- Energy and origin data: the share of purchased electricity versus fuels at the production site, whether electricity uses supplier-specific or grid-average factors, and the plant location.
- Data source tier: measured site data versus industry databases versus literature, per life cycle stage.
- Verification: third-party or critical review statement, and the reviewing party.

Suppliers that answer readily are signaling a managed quality system; suppliers that respond with a brochure-level sustainability statement are signaling that the number will be a placeholder in your inventory.

## The Standards Behind the Numbers: GHG Protocol, ISO 14067, PEF

Standards exist so that two footprints of the same product mean roughly the same thing. The GHG Protocol defines Scopes 1–3 at corporate level, and its Corporate Value Chain (Scope 3) Standard defines Category 1 purchased goods, requiring cradle-to-gate results for products suppliers sell downstream; its Product Standard governs per-product footprints. ISO 14067 is the dedicated product carbon footprint standard, defining the functional unit, system boundary, biogenic carbon treatment and reporting requirements — a supplier claiming ISO 14067 alignment should be able to show which parts of the study follow it. The EU Product Environmental Footprint (PEF) method matters where a customer or category association asks for multi-indicator studies, in which carbon is one of up to sixteen indicators.

You do not need every supplier on the same standard this year. You do need to record which standard each number came from, because a GHG Protocol Category 1 figure and an ISO 14067 product footprint can differ in boundary and allocation even for identical production.

## Judging Data Quality: Primary Versus Secondary Sources

The distance between a defensible footprint and a rough one is data quality. Rank supplier answers on a three-tier scale. Tier 1 — primary: site-measured energy, actual yields and supplier-specific raw material data for the grade you buy; this is what a serious study contains for the largest stages. Tier 2 — adapted secondary: industry-average datasets (for soybean cultivation or electricity, say) combined with real site data for conversion steps; acceptable for most purposes and common in practice. Tier 3 — fully secondary: a generic database value applied without site data, fine as a screening estimate but weak as a basis for comparison or improvement tracking.

![Analyst comparing carbon footprint study reports from different suppliers at a desk with an emissions chart on screen](/assets/images/resource-ps-phosphatidylserine-carbon-footprint-scope3-data-us-eu-detail.webp)

Two traps deserve attention. Biogenic and land-use effects on crop-derived inputs can dominate or mask the fossil contribution depending on method — ask how land-use change is handled. And allocation among co-products can shift a PS number by double-digit percentages; if two suppliers differ sharply, check their allocation choices before concluding anything about their plants.

## Comparing Suppliers Without Comparing Apples to Orchards

Once the packs arrive, normalize before you rank. Same functional unit first: convert everything to kg CO2e per kg of pure PS content (adjust for 50% versus 70% grades) or per thousand servings at your dose — not per kg of sold powder with different carriers. Same boundary second: flag suppliers whose figure excludes packaging, inland transport or crop stages, and request the missing stages or mark the comparison provisional. Same allocation third: where methods differ, ask each supplier to restate the figure under your preferred method; most can, since the underlying inventory supports it. Materiality fourth: for PS, crop cultivation and processing energy usually dominate, so a supplier improving its electricity mix moves the number in a way packaging tweaks do not.

Treat carbon as one column in the sourcing matrix alongside price, quality capability, lead time and reliability. A Tier 1 footprint from a supplier costing 4% more than a Tier 3 competitor can be worth exactly that in reduced reporting risk — especially for EU brands whose Scope 3 disclosures increasingly face assurance.

## Using the Data in Decisions and in Marketing

Internal use is where the data earns its keep: baseline the inventory, set supplier engagement targets, and track reductions across annual reviews. External use is where restraint pays. Under the EU Empowering Consumers Directive, generic environmental claims without recognized excellent performance, and claims based on offsetting presented as a product attribute, are restricted — and consumer-protection pressure is tightening in the US too. A claim such as "PS sourced from a supplier with a verified cradle-to-gate footprint of X kg CO2e/kg," with the study boundary and year stated, is defensible; "our product is carbon neutral," built on offsets, is now a liability rather than a marketing line. Where a claim is wanted, put the study behind it, state the standard and boundary, and keep the claim at ingredient level unless you hold product-level evidence.

## FAQ

### Q: What carbon data should I request from an ingredient supplier?

A: A product carbon footprint in kg CO2e per kg of product, cradle-to-gate, with the boundary statement, allocation method, study year, per-stage data sources (primary versus database), electricity origin and any third-party verification — six items, ideally on a one-page request template.

### Q: What does cradle-to-gate kg CO2e per kg mean?

A: Emissions from raw material production (for PS, typically crop cultivation and oil extraction) through the supplier's manufacturing gate, per kilogram of product. It covers the life cycle stages the supplier controls, which is why it is the right unit for Scope 3 Category 1.

### Q: Which standard governs product carbon footprint claims?

A: ISO 14067 is the dedicated product carbon footprint standard; the GHG Protocol Scope 3 and Product Standards govern corporate and value-chain accounting, and the EU PEF method applies where multi-indicator studies are requested. Record which standard each supplier figure follows.

### Q: How does Scope 3 Category 1 apply to purchased ingredients?

A: Category 1 covers emissions from products you purchase — for food and supplement brands usually 70–90% of the total inventory. Ingredients like phosphatidylserine contribute through their cradle-to-gate footprint multiplied by purchased volumes.

### Q: Can we market our product as low-carbon because of the PS ingredient?

A: Only with care. Under the EU Empowering Consumers Directive, unsubstantiated generic claims and offset-based "carbon neutral" product claims are restricted. Make ingredient-level claims with the study, standard, boundary and year stated, and have claims reviewed before release.

## Conclusion

Supplier carbon data is becoming a standard procurement input, and phosphatidylserine buyers are better off collecting it deliberately than under deadline. A one-page request — footprint per kg, boundary, allocation, data tier, verification — yields numbers that can be compared, defended and tracked; anything vaguer yields placeholder rows in an inventory that increasingly faces assurance. Normalize for functional unit and boundary before ranking suppliers, weigh the result in the sourcing matrix alongside quality and price, and keep external claims as specific as the data behind them. The buyers who build this muscle now will find every future reporting cycle cheaper than the last.
