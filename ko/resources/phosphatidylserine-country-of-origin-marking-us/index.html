Phosphatidylserine Country of Origin Marking Checklist for US Importers

Learn how U.S. buyers should review country-of-origin marking on imported phosphatidylserine drums, what to check when a U.S. address appears, and when.

출판 July 16, 2026

PS 제품 보기규격서 요청

U.S. phosphatidylserine buyers often review the technical and regulatory file in a familiar order: specification, COA path, food-facility registration, prior notice timing, FSVP ownership, and incoming release. Then a smaller-looking question appears on the actual shipment units: is the country-of-origin marking on the phosphatidylserine drum acceptable for U.S. import and downstream handling, or is everyone assuming someone else checked it?

The short answer is that imported phosphatidylserine should reach the U.S. buyer with a clear, conspicuous country-of-origin indication on the article or its required container path, and that review should happen as its own shipment-control task, not as a side note inside packaging or customs emails. The governing framework sits with U.S. Customs and Border Protection. Under 19 CFR 134.11, unless an exception applies, every article of foreign origin or its container imported into the United States must be marked in a conspicuous place, legibly, indelibly, and permanently enough to indicate to the ultimate purchaser in the United States the English name of the country of origin. FDA's dietary supplement labeling guide points buyers in the same direction by stating that, unless excepted by law, every foreign-origin article or its container imported into the United States must conspicuously indicate the English name of the country of origin, while FDA's own import page separately explains that importers are responsible for ensuring food products are safe, sanitary, and labeled according to U.S. requirements.

That creates a practical buyer lesson. Country-of-origin marking is not the same question as whether the phosphatidylserine supplier is approved, whether prior notice was filed, whether the FSVP importer was identified correctly, or whether the drum liner has a usable food-contact declaration. It is a narrower but still important question: can a U.S. purchaser, receiver, distributor, or downstream customer tell the actual origin of the imported phosphatidylserine from the article or container path that reaches them?

This article is written for U.S. ingredient importers, distributors, supplement manufacturers, procurement managers, warehouse teams, QA reviewers, and broker-facing coordinators handling phosphatidylserine. It focuses on shipment labeling, receiving control, repacking escalation, and customer-file clarity. It does not provide legal advice and it does not make medical treatment claims.

구매자가 먼저 알아야 할 간단한 답변

If your team is importing phosphatidylserine into the United States, the simplest workable country-of-origin workflow is:

  1. Lock the exact phosphatidylserine route, consignee structure, and shipment stage first.
  2. Check whether the foreign-origin article itself or the usual container that reaches the ultimate purchaser is carrying the English country name clearly.
  3. Confirm the marking is conspicuous, readable, and durable enough to remain through storage, movement, and receipt.
  4. Recheck the label immediately if a U.S. importer, distributor, or customer address appears on the container.
  5. Escalate if the material will be repacked or relabeled after entry, rather than assuming the original marking logic carries forward automatically.

That is the operational line buyers need. Country-of-origin marking is not a substitute for prior notice, FSVP importer mapping, 포장 선언 검토, 또는 incoming warehouse release. It is its own control point because one missing or misleading origin statement can create avoidable friction even when the rest of the import file looks organized.

Why Country-of-Origin Marking Is Its Own Buyer Task

Customs marking is different from FDA supplier approval and shipment filing

Many phosphatidylserine teams lump all import controls together because the same people often exchange the documents. The official sources do not support that shortcut.

CBP's marking framework under 19 CFR Part 134 governs country-of-origin marking. FDA's import page separately says that food importers are responsible for ensuring products are safe, sanitary, and labeled according to U.S. requirements, and that imported food products are subject to FDA inspection when offered for import. That means a phosphatidylserine shipment can have a usable prior-notice file, a named FSVP importer, and a technically acceptable specification packet, while still needing a separate check on whether the foreign origin is being shown properly on the article or container that reaches the U.S. purchaser.

For buyers, the distinction looks like this:

구매자 질문답을 찾는 데 도움이 되는 파일스스로 증명하지 못하는 것들
Who owns supplier verification at entry?FSVP importer fileWhether the drum or usual container is origin-marked correctly
Did the shipment move into FDA arrival filing correctly?Prior notice and entry handoffWhether the receiver can identify the product's country of origin from the shipment unit
Is the ingredient technically acceptable?Specification, COA, QA reviewWhether the customs marking is conspicuous and durable
Is the packaging contact layer acceptable?Packaging declaration supportWhether the container tells the ultimate purchaser the actual country of origin

The practical implication is an inference from those sources: U.S. phosphatidylserine buyers should treat country-of-origin review as a shipment-label task that sits beside the customs and FDA file, not inside it.

U.S. distributor wording can create preventable marking mistakes

The second reason this topic matters is that many imported ingredient containers carry U.S.-side commercial wording. A drum may show an importer name, distributor name, warehouse code, or customer-specific label overlay. That is where preventable confusion often starts.

Under 19 CFR 134.22(c), containers of imported merchandise bearing the name and address of an importer, distributor, or other U.S. person must be marked in close proximity to the U.S. address so the country of origin of the contents is clear. The broader marking rules in Subpart E also require the origin statement to be legible, conspicuous, and permanent enough to remain until the ultimate purchaser receives it, and they require close-proximity origin wording such as "Made in" or "Product of" when U.S. wording could mislead the purchaser about actual origin.

For phosphatidylserine buyers, that creates a narrow but important operational question: if a U.S. address or customer-facing distribution label appears on the drum, does the origin statement remain clear enough that no warehouse, contract manufacturer, or downstream buyer mistakes the item for a U.S.-origin product? That question should be answered before the drums are released to domestic inventory.

The Six-Part Workflow for Reviewing PS Country-of-Origin Marking

Lock the exact phosphatidylserine route and shipment stage first

Before anyone inspects the label, fix the commercial item in writing. Is the shipment general phosphatidylserine, a soy route, or a sunflower route? Is the stage first import, repeat order, distributor resale, or contract-manufacturer handoff? Will the drums go straight to the importer, to a third-party warehouse, or to a downstream manufacturer?

This matters because country-of-origin review becomes vague when teams inspect "PS drums" without fixing which route and which handoff path they are discussing. The receiving site may be looking at one label format while procurement approved another.

At minimum, the review note should identify:

  • 정확한 제품 경로
  • consignee or warehouse path
  • whether the review is pre-shipment, arrival-stage, or post-receipt
  • whether the label carries supplier-only wording or added U.S. commercial wording
  • whether any downstream relabeling or repacking is planned

That note gives customs, QA, warehouse, and sales teams one shared reference point before the label question turns into an argument about who was supposed to check it.

Check whether the article or its usual container is carrying the origin clearly

Once the route is fixed, move to the actual marking rule. Under 19 CFR 134.11, every foreign-origin article or its container must show the English name of the country of origin unless an exception applies. Under 19 CFR 134.22, when the article itself is excepted from marking, the outermost container in which the article ordinarily reaches the ultimate purchaser must be origin-marked.

That means buyers should not ask the origin question abstractly. They should ask it against the unit that actually reaches the relevant purchaser stage.

Shipment situationWhat the buyer should confirm
Imported phosphatidylserine arrives in its standard drum and that drum is what the U.S. receiver buys or receivesCheck whether the drum or required article/container path clearly shows the country of origin
The product is handled through a distributor before reaching a supplement manufacturerCheck which container is the "usual container" reaching that purchaser stage
The material is expected to move into another labeled container laterConfirm whether the current container marking is enough only up to that point and whether later remarking must be planned

This is also why country-of-origin review should not be collapsed into MOQ 및 포장 계획. Standard drum quantity and label legality are different controls.

Review wording, placement, and permanence before the drums move inland

The next step is not just "is there an origin line?" It is "is the line usable?"

CBP's rules require origin marking to be conspicuous, legible, and permanent enough for the article or required container path. Subpart E further states that other acceptable marking methods are allowed if they ensure the country of origin conspicuously appears, and that sticker labels or tags must be attached securely enough to remain until the ultimate purchaser receives the item unless deliberately removed.

For phosphatidylserine buyers, the practical checklist is:

  1. Is the origin shown in English?
  2. Can a receiver find it quickly without opening records or asking sales?
  3. Is it likely to remain readable through shipment, storage, and receiving?
  4. Is the wording clear, such as "Made in China" or "Product of China," rather than vague distribution language?
  5. Is the origin label being obscured by warehouse stickers, pallet labels, or customer routing overlays?

The safest commercial practice is to review the same label presentation that will actually reach the U.S. receiver, not a cropped artwork sample from an earlier approval cycle.

Escalate immediately when a U.S. address, city, or customer label appears

This is the step many buyers skip because the container "already has the origin on it somewhere." That is not always enough.

When a U.S. importer, distributor, or warehouse address appears on the drum, 19 CFR 134.22(c) requires origin marking in close proximity to that U.S. address so the contents' origin is clear. The CBP marking rules in Subpart E also state that when U.S. wording or another non-origin location could mislead the ultimate purchaser, the actual country of origin must appear legibly and permanently in close proximity and in comparable size, preceded by wording such as "Made in" or "Product of."

The buyer lesson is simple: do not approve a phosphatidylserine label simply because the country appears somewhere on the package. Recheck the layout if any of these appear:

  • U.S. importer address
  • U.S. distributor address
  • U.S. warehouse city or state
  • private-label customer address
  • overlay sticker applied after customs release

At that point, the review question is not only "is origin present?" It is "could the added U.S. wording mislead the purchaser about origin if we leave the label as-is?"

Separate import-stage marking from post-entry repacking or relabeling

The next mistake is assuming the import-stage marking logic survives every downstream handling step. It may not.

Under 19 CFR 134.26, if an article subject to marking is intended to be repacked in retail containers after release from Customs custody, or CBP has reason to believe that will happen, the importer must provide a certification and must ensure the new container does not conceal the origin marking or else that the new container is properly marked. FDA's country-of-origin policy guide also notes that repackers are required by CBP to mark containers of repackaged imports with the English name of the country of origin, while FDA focuses on avoiding false or misleading geographical-origin claims under the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act.

For phosphatidylserine buyers, the safest reading is practical: if the material will be relabeled, split, or repacked after entry, do not assume the first drum label closes the topic. Instead, ask a narrower follow-up question:

  • Is the repacking only a warehouse handling step, or does a new purchaser-facing container appear?
  • Will the origin wording remain visible after the new label is applied?
  • Does the downstream commercial path require a new container-level origin statement?
  • Does the planned relabeling create a misleading U.S.-origin impression?

In many B2B phosphatidylserine projects, the correct answer is to escalate the remarking question to the importer, customs broker, or compliance counsel before the domestic label is finalized. That is better than discovering the problem after stock has already been redistributed.

Common Mistakes in Phosphatidylserine Origin-Marking Review

The repeated mistakes are usually workflow mistakes rather than customs-theory mistakes.

  1. Treating country-of-origin marking as if prior notice or FSVP already covered it.
  2. Approving a drum label because origin appears somewhere on the container, without checking proximity to a U.S. address.
  3. Letting warehouse or customer overlay labels obscure the original origin statement.
  4. Assuming import-stage marking is still sufficient after repacking or purchaser-facing relabeling.
  5. Confusing a truthful "imported by" line with a complete country-of-origin marking.
  6. Reviewing a packaging declaration file and thinking that also answers the origin-marking question.

The practical fix is narrow: keep one question open until it is actually closed. What label or container presentation will the U.S. purchaser see, and does that presentation clearly state the true country of origin?

자주 묻는 질문

Does imported phosphatidylserine need country-of-origin marking in the United States?

Yes, unless an exception applies. CBP's marking rules require every foreign-origin article or its container imported into the United States to indicate the English name of the country of origin.

Is country-of-origin marking the same thing as prior notice or FSVP?

No. Prior notice, FSVP, and origin marking are separate import-control questions. A phosphatidylserine shipment can have valid prior-notice and FSVP handling but still need an independent check on container marking.

What if the drum shows a U.S. importer or distributor address?

That is a separate review trigger. CBP's rules require the country of origin to appear clearly in close proximity when a U.S. importer or distributor address is shown on the container.

Can a distributor rely on an "Imported by" line without showing the country?

No. A truthful importer or distributor line is not the same as a complete country-of-origin statement. Buyers should still confirm that the actual country of origin is stated clearly and conspicuously.

What if imported phosphatidylserine will be repacked after entry?

Do not assume the original drum marking ends the review. CBP's repacking rules can create additional certification or container-marking requirements, so the importer should review the exact downstream repacking plan before approving the new label path.

결론

Phosphatidylserine country-of-origin marking becomes manageable when buyers stop treating it as a customs footnote and start treating it as a defined receiving-label control.

The strongest U.S. workflow is practical: lock the exact route and shipment stage first, confirm whether the article or usual container is carrying the English country name clearly, recheck any label that also shows U.S. distribution wording, escalate any repacking or relabeling plan, and close the file with one clear decision before the drums move downstream. That approach helps importers, distributors, warehouses, and contract manufacturers avoid a simple but expensive failure pattern: the ingredient file looks complete, but the shipment unit still does not communicate origin clearly enough to the U.S. purchaser.
